Respiratory distress in a newborn means RR > 60/min with any of: grunting, nasal flaring, chest recession (subcostal, intercostal, sternal) or cyanosis. It is one of the commonest reasons for admission to the neonatal unit. The cause is often outside the lungs (heart, sepsis, hypoglycaemia, hypothermia), so every baby with distress needs a search for the cause as well as oxygen.

Causes of respiratory distress in the newborn

LungsTTN (the commonest at term), RDS (the commonest in preterm), meconium aspiration, pneumonia, pneumothorax and pulmonary hypoplasia
AirwayBilateral choanal atresia (newborns breathe through the nose, so the baby is pink only when crying), Pierre Robin sequence, oesophageal atresia with fistula (frothy saliva, NGT coils in the pouch)
SurgicalCongenital diaphragmatic hernia · congenital lobar emphysema
HeartCyanotic congenital heart disease · heart failure · PPHN
OthersSepsis; hypoglycaemia, hypothermia and metabolic acidosis; anaemia and polycythaemia; birth asphyxia and intracranial bleeding

Respiratory distress syndrome (hyaline membrane disease)

  • Cause: surfactant deficiency (made by type II pneumocytes, mature at 35 weeks) → alveoli collapse at the end of expiration, so each breath must re-open them and the baby tires
  • Risk ↑: prematurity (the main one), infant of a diabetic mother, male, second twin, elective caesarean section, asphyxia, hypothermia
  • Risk ↓: antenatal steroids, prolonged rupture of membranes, IUGR, maternal hypertension (fetal stress ↑ cortisol)
  • Clinical: distress within 4 hours of birth, worsening over 48–72 h, then improving as the baby starts making surfactant; grunting (expiration against a closed glottis keeps the alveoli open) and reduced air entry
  • Chest X-ray: diffuse ground-glass (reticulogranular) lungs with air bronchograms and small lung volume; "white-out" if severe
  • Group B streptococcal pneumonia looks the same clinically and on X-ray, so take a blood culture and start antibiotics
Respiratory supportCPAP (6–8 cm H2O) from birth is the first choice, because the constant pressure keeps the alveoli open; ventilation if CPAP fails; target SpO2 90–94% (higher oxygen damages the retina and lungs)
SurfactantNatural surfactant (poractant 200 mg/kg) by tube or thin catheter (LISA) when oxygen need rises above 30% on CPAP; a second dose may be given if the oxygen need stays high
SupportiveThermal care, IV fluids and glucose, ampicillin + gentamicin until cultures are negative, caffeine in very preterm babies
Monitoring the responseFirst sign of improvement: falling oxygen need and less recession. Targets: SpO2 90–94%, normal blood gas. Signs of failure on CPAP: oxygen need above 40%, recurrent apnoea, rising PaCO2 or acidosis → surfactant and ventilation. A sudden deterioration suggests a pneumothorax
ComplicationsPneumothorax, PDA, IVH, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy (oxygen)

Pneumothorax

  • Follows ventilation or bag-and-mask, RDS or meconium aspiration (stiff lungs and high pressure rupture the alveoli); sudden deterioration, reduced air entry on one side, shift of the apex beat
  • Bright transillumination of the chest; X-ray: black lung field without markings and mediastinal shift
  • Tension pneumothorax: needle aspiration (2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line), then chest drain

Congenital diaphragmatic hernia

  • Bowel herniates into the chest, usually left posterolateral (Bochdalek); the compressed lung does not grow, so pulmonary hypoplasia decides survival
  • Severe distress at birth, scaphoid abdomen (the bowel is in the chest), heart sounds on the right and bowel sounds in the chest; often seen on antenatal ultrasound
  • X-ray: bowel loops in the left chest, mediastinum pushed right
  • Intubate at once: bag-and-mask air fills the bowel in the chest and compresses the lung further. Pass an NGT to decompress the stomach, stabilise, then repair surgically

Cyanosis

  • Central: blue tongue and lips, seen when reduced Hb is above 5 g/dL; always abnormal
  • Peripheral (acrocyanosis): blue hands and feet with a pink tongue; normal in the first day, otherwise due to cold or shock
  • Causes of central cyanosis:
    • lungs: RDS, MAS, pneumonia, pneumothorax, CDH
    • cyanotic heart disease: TGA, TOF, pulmonary atresia, TAPVR
    • PPHN, polycythaemia, methaemoglobinaemia
Hyperoxia test
  • Give 100% oxygen for 10 minutes and measure right radial PaO2
  • PaO2 rises above 150 mmHg → lung disease
  • Stays below 100 mmHg → cyanotic heart disease (or severe PPHN) → echocardiography
Clues to heart diseaseCyanosis without much distress, a murmur or weak femoral pulses, no rise in SpO2 with oxygen, and worsening as the duct closes (day 2–5)
Duct-dependent lesionProstaglandin E1 infusion 5–10 ng/kg/min (higher if the duct is closing) keeps the duct open, so blood can mix until surgery; side effect: apnoea (be ready to ventilate); refer to a cardiac centre
PPHNPulmonary pressure stays high after birth → right-to-left shunt through the duct and foramen ovale; follows MAS, asphyxia, sepsis or CDH; labile oxygen, pre-ductal SpO2 higher than post-ductal; ventilation and inhaled nitric oxide (a pulmonary vasodilator)

Apnoea

  • Pause in breathing ≥ 20 seconds, or shorter with bradycardia or cyanosis; it matters because repeated hypoxia injures the brain
  • Apnoea of prematurity: immature respiratory centre, from day 2, a diagnosis of exclusion
  • Secondary: sepsis, meningitis, hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia, hypothermia, IVH, seizures, anaemia, reflux, maternal drugs
  • Gentle stimulation, clear the airway, bag-and-mask if not breathing
  • Look for and treat the cause (septic screen, glucose)
  • Caffeine citrate for apnoea of prematurity (stimulates the respiratory centre); CPAP or ventilation if recurrent
  • Response: fewer and shorter apnoea episodes on the monitor; caffeine is usually stopped at about 34 weeks when the baby is free of apnoea
Exam trap
  • Apnoea in a term baby is never "apnoea of prematurity": look for sepsis, seizures or hypoglycaemia.
  • Acrocyanosis with a pink tongue on day 1 is normal; a blue tongue is not.
