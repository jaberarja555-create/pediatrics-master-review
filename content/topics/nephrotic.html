Nephrotic syndrome is a disorder of the glomerular filtration barrier that lets large amounts of protein leak into the urine. In children it is usually idiopathic and steroid-sensitive (minimal change disease), peak age 2–6 years, boys more than girls. Most relapse but keep normal kidney function.

Definition (all of)

  • Heavy proteinuria: urine dipstick 3+ or more, or urine protein : creatinine ratio > 200 mg/mmol (> 2 mg/mg), or > 40 mg/m²/h
  • Hypoalbuminaemia: albumin < 25 g/L
  • Oedema
  • Usually with hyperlipidaemia (liver makes more lipoproteins as albumin falls)
  • Mechanism of oedema: low oncotic pressure + sodium retention by the kidney

Causes

GroupCauses
Primary (idiopathic, 90%)
  • Minimal change disease (≈ 80% under 6 years: normal on light microscopy, effaced podocytes on EM)
  • FSGS (steroid-resistant)
  • Membranoproliferative, membranous
Secondary
  • HSP, SLE, post-infectious GN
  • Infections: hepatitis B, HIV, malaria (P. malariae), schistosomiasis
  • Drugs
  • Amyloid
  • Sickle cell
Congenital (< 3 months)Finnish type (nephrin) · congenital syphilis, CMV

Clinical features

  • Periorbital oedema (morning, often mistaken for allergy) → generalised pitting oedema: legs, scrotum/labia, ascites, pleural effusion
  • Frothy urine, reduced urine volume, weight gain
  • Abdominal pain, diarrhoea (gut oedema), breathlessness
Features of minimal change
  • Age 1–10 years
  • Normal BP, no macroscopic haematuria, normal renal function, normal C3
  • Selective proteinuria (albumin)

Investigations

  • Urine: dipstick, UPCR (early morning) · microscopy (RBCs, casts)
  • Blood: albumin, cholesterol (↑), urea, creatinine, electrolytes, CBC (↑ Hct: hypovolaemia) · C3/C4, ASO
  • When atypical:
    • Hepatitis B, HIV, ANA, VDRL
    • Mantoux and CXR before steroids (TB in Sudan)
    • Stool for parasites (strongyloides)
  • Renal biopsy only if: age < 1 or > 12 years, steroid resistance, persistent hypertension, macroscopic haematuria, low C3, renal failure

Treatment of the first episode

  1. Prednisolone 60 mg/m²/day (or 2 mg/kg/day, max 60 mg) once daily for 4–6 weeks, then 40 mg/m² (1.5 mg/kg) on alternate days for 4–6 weeks, then stop (total 8–12 weeks)
  2. No-added-salt diet (normal protein) · fluid restriction only if severe oedema with low Na
  3. Penicillin V prophylaxis while oedematous (peritonitis) · pneumococcal vaccine
  4. Diuretics (furosemide) only for severe oedema, and with care (thrombosis, hypovolaemia) · 20% albumin 0.5–1 g/kg over 4 h + furosemide for refractory oedema
  5. Daily weight and urine dipstick · BP · teach parents to test urine at home

Response definitions

RemissionUrine dipstick negative or trace for 3 consecutive days (usually within 7–14 days)
Steroid-sensitiveRemission within 4 weeks of daily prednisolone (≈ 90% of children)
Steroid-resistant
  • No remission after 4–6 weeks of daily prednisolone → biopsy (often FSGS)
  • Calcineurin inhibitors (ciclosporin, tacrolimus), ACE inhibitor
Relapse
  • Dipstick ≥ 3+ for 3 days
  • Treat with prednisolone 60 mg/m² until remission for 3 days, then alternate-day 40 mg/m² for 4 weeks
Frequent relapses / steroid-dependent
  • ≥ 2 relapses in 6 months (or ≥ 4 in a year)
  • Relapse on or within 2 weeks of stopping steroids → levamisole, cyclophosphamide, MMF, ciclosporin, rituximab

Complications

  • Infection: spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (pneumococcus), cellulitis, sepsis (loss of IgG, steroids)
  • Hypovolaemia: abdominal pain, cold peripheries, ↑ Hct → albumin, fluids
  • Thrombosis (hypercoagulable: loss of antithrombin III): renal vein, cerebral venous, DVT
  • AKI (hypovolaemia, diuretics)
  • Steroid side effects: Cushingoid face, growth slowing, hypertension, cataract, behaviour, osteoporosis
  • Hyperlipidaemia · malnutrition
