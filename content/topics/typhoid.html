Typhoid fever is a systemic infection by Salmonella Typhi (and Paratyphi), spread by the faeco-oral route through contaminated water and food. It is common in Sudan. The bacteria invade the Peyer's patches of the ileum and spread in the blood; the dangerous complications (perforation, bleeding) come in the third week.

Clinical features

Week 1Step-ladder fever, headache, malaise, abdominal pain, constipation (or diarrhoea in young children), dry cough · relative bradycardia
Week 2High sustained fever, toxic, coated tongue, rose spots (on the trunk, rare in dark skin), splenomegaly, hepatomegaly, abdominal distension, "pea-soup" diarrhoea, delirium ("typhoid state")
Week 3Complications: intestinal perforation (ileum: sudden pain, peritonitis), GI bleeding, myocarditis, encephalopathy, hepatitis, cholecystitis, osteomyelitis (sickle cell)

Children under 5 often have a non-specific febrile illness with diarrhoea and vomiting. Chronic carriers (gallbladder) keep the disease in the community.

Investigations

  • Blood culture: the test of choice in week 1 (positive in 60–80%)
  • Bone marrow culture: most sensitive (> 90%), even after antibiotics
  • Stool and urine culture: positive from week 2–3 (carriers)
  • Widal test (O and H agglutinins): rises from week 2; many false positives in endemic areas (single titre unreliable; a 4-fold rise is significant)
  • CBC: leucopenia, relative lymphocytosis, anaemia, low platelets

Treatment

SituationDrug
UncomplicatedAzithromycin 10–20 mg/kg once daily × 7 days (first choice) · or cefixime 10 mg/kg 12-hourly × 10–14 days · ciprofloxacin where sensitive
Severe / complicated / vomitingCeftriaxone 75–100 mg/kg/day IV × 10–14 days
Encephalopathy, shockAdd dexamethasone 3 mg/kg, then 1 mg/kg 6-hourly × 48 h
PerforationResuscitate, broad-spectrum antibiotics (ceftriaxone + metronidazole), laparotomy

Fever takes 4–7 days to settle after starting treatment. Prevention: safe water, sanitation, hand washing, typhoid conjugate vaccine (TCV) from 6 months.

Brucellosis is a zoonosis caught from raw (unboiled) milk and dairy products or contact with infected animals (goats, sheep, camels, cattle). It is common in Sudan's pastoral communities. Brucella melitensis (goats, sheep) is the commonest. It causes a prolonged, relapsing fever and is an important cause of PUO.

Clinical features

  • Fever (often undulant, evening), drenching sweats (malodorous), rigors
  • Fatigue, headache, anorexia, weight loss
  • Arthralgia, arthritis (hip, knee, sacroiliitis)
  • Hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy
  • Complications: spondylitis, osteomyelitis, endocarditis (main cause of death), neurobrucellosis, orchitis

Diagnosis

  • Serology: standard tube agglutination (SAT) ≥ 1:160 (or 4-fold rise), Rose Bengal screening, ELISA
  • Blood culture (slow, needs prolonged incubation) · bone marrow culture more sensitive
  • CBC: normal or low WBC, relative lymphocytosis · ↑ ESR, LFT

Treatment

AgeRegimenDuration
< 8 yearsCotrimoxazole TMP 10 mg/kg/day in 2 doses + rifampicin 15–20 mg/kg/day (± gentamicin 5 mg/kg/day for the first 7–14 days)6 weeks
≥ 8 yearsDoxycycline 2–4 mg/kg/day (max 200 mg) + rifampicin (or + gentamicin)6 weeks

Doxycycline is avoided under 8 years (tooth staining). Monotherapy and short courses cause relapse. Prevention: boil milk, animal vaccination.
