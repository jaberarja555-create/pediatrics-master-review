Stopping bleeding needs vessels, platelets (primary plug) and clotting factors (fibrin clot). The type of bleeding tells which part has failed; four simple tests (platelet count, PT, APTT, bleeding time) find the defect.

Causes of thrombocytopenia

  • Destruction (marrow full of megakaryocytes): ITP, SLE, HIV, drugs · consumption: DIC, HUS, sepsis, malaria, giant haemangioma (Kasabach–Merritt) · hypersplenism (kala-azar, portal hypertension)
  • Newborn: maternal ITP or alloimmune (antibodies cross the placenta), sepsis, TORCH infection
  • Low production (few megakaryocytes): leukaemia, neuroblastoma in the marrow, aplastic anaemia, Fanconi anaemia · chemotherapy · TAR syndrome (absent radius, thumbs present) · Wiskott–Aldrich (X-linked: small platelets, eczema, infections)
Best next step · child with bruising
  • CBC with blood film, then PT and APTT; ask about family history, drugs (aspirin) and whether the bruises fit the story (non-accidental injury).

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is the commonest cause of a low platelet count in a well child: IgG autoantibodies coat the platelets and the spleen removes them. Peak age 2–5 years, boys and girls equally; a viral infection 1–4 weeks before in most.

FeaturesA well child with sudden petechiae and bruises ("fine yesterday") · nose and gum bleeding · no pallor (unless much bleeding), no lymph nodes, no big liver or spleen, no bone pain · intracranial haemorrhage in < 1%
TestsCBC: isolated low platelets (often < 20 × 10⁹/L), normal Hb and WBC · film: large platelets, no blasts · PT, APTT normal
Bone marrowNot needed in typical ITP · do it if anything is atypical: anaemia, abnormal WBC or blasts, big liver, spleen or nodes, bone pain, fever, or ITP that does not respond · shows many megakaryocytes
CourseNewly diagnosed < 3 months · persistent 3–12 months · chronic > 12 months · about 80% of children recover within 6–12 months, most within weeks · chronic ITP is commoner in adolescents (check SLE, HIV)

Management

  • Skin bleeding only (bruises, petechiae), whatever the count: observation at home · explain to the parents · avoid aspirin, NSAIDs, IM injections and contact sports · return at once with mucosal bleeding, headache or vomiting
  • Mucosal or troublesome bleeding: prednisolone 2–4 mg/kg/day (max 120 mg) for 5–7 days, or IVIG 0.8–1 g/kg once (fastest rise) · anti-D in Rh-positive children
  • Life-threatening bleeding (intracranial, severe GI): platelet transfusion + IV methylprednisolone 30 mg/kg/day + IVIG · emergency splenectomy rarely
  • Chronic ITP: thrombopoietin receptor agonists (eltrombopag), rituximab · splenectomy last (after 5 years; vaccines, penicillin)
  • Platelet transfusion is not used routinely: the antibodies destroy the new platelets
ASH 2019 · immune thrombocytopenia in children
  • For newly diagnosed ITP with no or only skin bleeding, use observation rather than drugs, whatever the platelet count, and manage as an outpatient.
  • For non-life-threatening mucosal bleeding, give a short course of corticosteroids (7 days or less) rather than IVIG or anti-D.
Exam trap
  • A big spleen, pallor, lymph nodes or bone pain with low platelets is not ITP until leukaemia is excluded by bone marrow examination.
  • Starting steroids without a marrow when the picture is atypical can mask leukaemia (partial treatment of ALL).

Haemophilia

TypesHaemophilia A: factor VIII deficiency (about 80%) · haemophilia B (Christmas disease): factor IX · both X-linked recessive: boys affected, mothers carriers, maternal uncles affected · one third are new mutations · haemophilia C (factor XI) is autosomal recessive and mild
SeveritySevere < 1% factor: spontaneous joint and muscle bleeds · moderate 1–5%: bleeds after minor injury · mild 5–40%: bleeds after surgery or major injury · severity is the same within a family
FeaturesNewborn: bleeding after circumcision, cephalhaematoma, rarely intracranial · crawling and walking: large bruises · haemarthrosis (the hallmark: knee, elbow, ankle: hot, swollen, painful, held bent) · muscle haematoma (iliopsoas: hip held flexed) · intracranial haemorrhage (the main cause of death)
TestsAPTT long · PT, platelets and bleeding time normal · factor VIII or IX assay (diagnostic) · screen for inhibitors (antibodies against the given factor) · carrier testing of female relatives
Treatment of a bleedGive the factor at once (even before tests if a known haemophiliac): factor VIII dose (IU) = weight (kg) × desired rise (%) ÷ 2 (1 IU/kg raises it 2%; factor IX 1 IU/kg raises 1%) · joint or muscle: raise to 40–60% · head, throat, surgery: 80–100% · fresh frozen plasma or cryoprecipitate only if no concentrate · joint: rest, ice, compression, elevation, then physiotherapy
Other drugsDesmopressin (DDAVP) releases stored factor VIII: mild haemophilia A only (not B) · tranexamic acid for mouth and nose bleeds (not haematuria)
Long-termProphylaxis (regular factor, or emicizumab SC for haemophilia A) from early childhood in severe disease prevents joint damage · no IM injections, no aspirin or NSAIDs · hepatitis B vaccine (SC) · dental care · treatment card · complications: chronic arthropathy, inhibitors, transfusion infections
World Federation of Hemophilia 2020 · haemophilia
  • Give children with severe haemophilia regular prophylaxis, started early, to prevent joint bleeds and arthropathy.
  • Treat a bleed with factor replacement as soon as possible, ideally within 2 hours, and avoid IM injections and NSAIDs.

Von Willebrand disease, DIC and vitamin K deficiency

Von Willebrand diseaseThe commonest inherited bleeding disorder · usually autosomal dominant (girls and boys) · von Willebrand factor (VWF) fixes platelets to the vessel wall and carries factor VIII · mucosal bleeding: nosebleeds, gums, menorrhagia, after tooth extraction · haemarthrosis only in severe type 3 · bleeding time long, APTT long or normal, low VWF antigen and activity (ristocetin cofactor), low factor VIII · desmopressin (type 1), tranexamic acid, VWF-containing concentrate
DICWidespread clotting uses up platelets and factors · causes: septic shock (meningococcus), severe malaria, snake bite, burns, acute leukaemia (promyelocytic), asphyxia · oozing from drip sites, purpura, gangrene · low platelets, long PT and APTT, low fibrinogen, high D-dimer, fragmented red cells · treat the cause, fresh frozen plasma, platelets, cryoprecipitate
Vitamin K deficiency bleedingFactors II, VII, IX, X · breastfed newborn without vitamin K at birth (3.1), or later with cholestasis (biliary atresia), malabsorption, antibiotics · long PT (then APTT), normal platelets · vitamin K 1 mg IV, FFP if bleeding is severe
