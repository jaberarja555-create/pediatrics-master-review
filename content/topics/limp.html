A limp or a painful joint is one of the commonest musculoskeletal complaints in children. Most causes are benign and settle in days, such as transient synovitis or minor trauma, but a few must never be missed because delay destroys the joint or threatens life: septic arthritis, osteomyelitis and leukaemia. The approach therefore asks first whether the child is febrile or unwell, then uses the age and the number of joints to narrow the cause.

History of a child with joint pain

The pain
  • Onset, duration and progression; one joint or several; migratory (moves from joint to joint) or fixed
  • Morning stiffness that eases with activity points to inflammatory arthritis; pain that worsens with use points to mechanical causes
  • Night pain that wakes the child points to tumour or leukaemia
Associated features
  • Fever, refusal to bear weight (infection)
  • Fatigue, weight loss, night sweats, pallor, bruising (malignancy, TB)
  • Rash, red eyes, diarrhoea, haematuria, abdominal pain (rheumatic and reactive causes)
Recent events
  • Sore throat 2–3 weeks before (rheumatic fever)
  • Viral illness (transient synovitis) · diarrhoea (reactive arthritis)
  • Trauma, and whether the story fits the injury (non-accidental injury)
Background
  • Sickle cell disease, haemophilia, contact with TB, raw milk (brucellosis)
  • Family history of arthritis, psoriasis or inflammatory bowel disease

Exam trap · hip pain felt in the knee

Hip disease (Perthes disease, SCFE) often presents with knee pain, because the hip and knee share the obturator and femoral nerve supply. A child with knee pain and a normal knee must have the hip examined.

Septic arthritis

Septic arthritis is bacterial infection of a joint space, usually by blood spread. It is a surgical emergency, because pus and bacterial enzymes destroy the joint cartilage within days, and in the hip the pressure can cut off the blood supply of the femoral head.

Organisms
  • Staphylococcus aureus at all ages (the commonest)
  • Kingella kingae in children under 4 years · group A streptococcus · Hib in the unvaccinated
  • Neonates: group B streptococcus, gram-negative bacilli · sickle cell disease: Salmonella
Features
  • Usually one large joint: hip and knee
  • High fever, toxic child, refuses to bear weight
  • Hot, swollen joint; any movement is very painful, so the hip is held flexed, abducted and externally rotated
  • Neonates may show only pseudoparalysis (not moving the limb) and irritability
Investigations
  • CBC, ESR and CRP, blood culture before antibiotics
  • Ultrasound of the hip shows the effusion
  • Joint aspiration (the key test): turbid fluid, white cells usually > 50 000/mm³, mostly neutrophils, Gram stain and culture
  • X-ray is normal early (baseline only)
Management of septic arthritis
  • Urgent drainage: aspiration or surgical washout (open drainage of the hip, because aspiration alone does not decompress it)
  • IV antibiotics started immediately after cultures: cloxacillin 50 mg/kg 6-hourly + ceftriaxone 80–100 mg/kg once daily (covers Kingella, Hib and Salmonella; use ceftriaxone in every child with sickle cell disease)
  • Analgesia, splint the joint for comfort, then early physiotherapy
  • Change to oral antibiotics when the child improves (usually after 3–5 days); total about 3 weeks
Monitoring the response
  • First signs of improvement: fever settles and pain falls within 48–72 hours; the child starts to move the limb
  • CRP falls by about half within 2–3 days and is followed to guide the switch to oral treatment and the end of treatment
  • Failure: fever or high CRP after 72 hours means undrained pus, an adjacent osteomyelitis or a resistant organism: repeat ultrasound or MRI, re-drain, review the culture

Acute osteomyelitis

  • Infection of bone, usually the metaphysis of long bones (femur, tibia), because its slow blood flow lets bacteria settle; Staph aureus commonest, Salmonella in sickle cell disease
  • Fever, point tenderness over the bone, refusal to use the limb; the joint itself moves fairly well (unlike septic arthritis)
  • X-ray is normal for the first 10–14 days (then periosteal reaction); MRI is the best early test; blood culture is positive in about half
  • IV antibiotics as for septic arthritis, then oral; total about 4–6 weeks; surgery for a subperiosteal abscess; complication: chronic osteomyelitis with sequestrum

Transient synovitis of the hip

Transient (toxic) synovitis is a self-limiting inflammation of the hip lining, usually after a viral upper respiratory infection, in children aged 3–10 years (boys more often). The child is well, afebrile or mildly febrile, limps but can usually bear some weight, and has normal or slightly raised blood markers. Treatment is rest and NSAIDs (ibuprofen 10 mg/kg 8-hourly); it settles within 1–2 weeks. The danger is to call septic arthritis transient synovitis.

Other causes of joint pain

Acute rheumatic feverMigratory polyarthritis of large joints, 2–3 weeks after a streptococcal sore throat, very painful, settles dramatically with aspirin (see 8.5)
Reactive arthritisSterile arthritis of the knees and ankles 1–4 weeks after a gut infection (Salmonella, Shigella, Campylobacter, Yersinia) or a urethritis; may come with conjunctivitis and urethritis (the classic triad); NSAIDs
IgA vasculitis (HSP)Painful swollen ankles and knees with palpable purpura on the legs and buttocks and abdominal pain (see 10.2)
HaemophiliaRecurrent haemarthrosis of the knee, elbow and ankle in a boy after minor trauma; replace the factor; never aspirate without factor cover (see 9.4)
LeukaemiaBone or joint pain (often at night) with pallor, bruising, hepatosplenomegaly and abnormal blood count; bone pain out of proportion to the joint signs (see 9.5)
TB and brucellosisChronic monoarthritis (hip, knee) or spine (Pott's disease, sacroiliitis in brucellosis), with weight loss and night sweats; raw milk points to brucellosis (see 5.5 and 5.8)
Familial Mediterranean feverEpisodes of fever with large-joint arthritis and abdominal pain lasting 1–3 days (see 13.4)

Growing pains

Growing pains are benign, recurrent leg pains of children aged 3–12 years. They are bilateral, felt in the thighs, calves or behind the knees, come in the evening or at night, are eased by massage, and are gone by the morning. The child never limps, and the examination and blood tests are normal. Reassurance and simple analgesia are enough.

Exam trap · red flags that exclude growing pains
  • Pain in one leg only, or in a joint rather than the muscles
  • A limp, swelling, warmth or reduced movement
  • Pain during the day or present in the morning
  • Fever, weight loss, pallor, bruising, or an abnormal blood count
