Bronchiolitis is an acute viral infection of the small airways (bronchioles) in infants under 2 years, peak 2–6 months. RSV causes most cases. Inflammation, mucus and debris block the bronchioles, causing air trapping and hypoxia. Treatment is supportive: oxygen and feeding; drugs do not help.

Causes and risk factors

  • Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV): 70–80%, winter/rainy season
  • Others: rhinovirus, parainfluenza, metapneumovirus, adenovirus, influenza
Risk of severe disease
  • Age < 3 months, prematurity, chronic lung disease
  • Congenital heart disease, immunodeficiency, neuromuscular disease
  • Not breastfed, smoke exposure, crowding

Clinical features

  • 1–3 days of coryza, then cough, fast breathing, wheeze, poor feeding
  • Tachypnoea, subcostal recession, nasal flaring, hyperinflated chest, liver pushed down
  • Fine inspiratory crackles and/or expiratory wheeze
  • Young infants: apnoea may be the first sign
  • Course: worst on days 3–5, cough lasts 2–3 weeks
  • Admit if: SpO₂ < 90–92%, feeding < 50–75% of normal, apnoea, severe distress, dehydration, risk factors
  • Diagnosis is clinical; CXR not routine (hyperinflation, patchy collapse)

Management

OxygenNasal prongs/cannula when SpO₂ < 90–92% · high-flow or CPAP if distress increases
Feeding and fluidsSmall frequent feeds · NG tube if not feeding · IV fluids (⅔ maintenance) if NG not tolerated
AirwayGentle nasal suction / saline drops before feeds
Not recommendedSalbutamol, steroids, antibiotics, adrenaline, chest physiotherapy, cough syrups (no benefit) · antibiotics only if secondary bacterial pneumonia suspected
DischargeSpO₂ ≥ 92% in air for 4–12 h, feeding > 75%, no apnoea

Prevention and outcome

  • Palivizumab (monthly anti-RSV antibody) or nirsevimab for high-risk infants where available · maternal RSV vaccine
  • Hand washing, breastfeeding, avoid smoke
  • Mortality low in healthy infants · up to 40% have recurrent wheeze later
  • Rare: bronchiolitis obliterans (adenovirus)
