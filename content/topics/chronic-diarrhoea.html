Persistent diarrhoea is diarrhoea that starts acutely and lasts 14 days or more; chronic diarrhoea lasts more than 4 weeks. In Sudan most persistent diarrhoea follows infection in a malnourished child (damaged villi, lactose intolerance, giardia, HIV). Malabsorption means poor absorption of nutrients, shown by bulky, pale, offensive stools and failure to thrive.

Investigations

  • Stool: microscopy (giardia, ova), culture, reducing substances and pH < 5.5 (carbohydrate malabsorption), fat globules, faecal elastase (pancreas)
  • CBC, albumin, iron, folate, Ca, vitamin D
  • Coeliac serology: anti-tTG IgA + total IgA · small bowel biopsy (gold standard)
  • Sweat chloride (CF) · HIV test · TSH

Management of persistent diarrhoea (IMCI / WHO)

  • Treat dehydration (ORS) · zinc 10–14 days · vitamin A, multivitamins
  • Feeding: continue breastfeeding · reduce animal milk to ≤ 50 mL/kg/day, mix it with cereal, or use yoghurt (less lactose) · lactose-free diet for 2 weeks if lactose intolerance is proven · small frequent energy-rich meals
  • Treat giardia (metronidazole) and any infection (UTI, pneumonia, otitis) · test for HIV
  • Severe persistent diarrhoea (with dehydration) or under 6 months: admit

Coeliac disease

  • Immune enteropathy triggered by gluten (gliadin) in wheat, barley, rye in genetically susceptible people (HLA-DQ2/DQ8) · common in Sudan (wheat bread, kisra)
  • Pathology: villous atrophy, crypt hyperplasia, intraepithelial lymphocytes (proximal small bowel)
  • Associations: type 1 DM, Down and Turner syndromes, IgA deficiency, autoimmune thyroid
  • Classic (6–24 months, after starting gluten): chronic diarrhoea, bulky offensive stools, abdominal distension, wasted buttocks, irritability, FTT
  • Older: short stature, iron-deficiency anaemia resistant to iron, delayed puberty, rickets, dermatitis herpetiformis, constipation
DiagnosisAnti-tTG IgA (on a gluten diet) + total IgA · anti-endomysial antibody · duodenal biopsy (gold standard): villous atrophy
TreatmentLifelong strict gluten-free diet (rice, maize, millet, sorghum, potato are safe) · iron, folate, vitamin D, calcium · dietitian
Follow-upSymptoms improve in weeks; tTG falls in 6–12 months · lymphoma risk if untreated

Cow's milk protein allergy (CMPA)

  • Immune reaction to cow's milk proteins, first months of life, mostly formula-fed (can occur in breastfed babies)
  • IgE-mediated (minutes): urticaria, angio-oedema, vomiting, wheeze, anaphylaxis
  • Non-IgE (hours–days): vomiting, reflux, diarrhoea, blood and mucus in stool (proctocolitis), eczema, FTT
  • Diagnosis: elimination for 2–4 weeks then re-challenge · skin prick / specific IgE for the immediate type
  • Treatment: extensively hydrolysed formula (amino-acid formula if severe) · breastfeeding mother avoids dairy · not goat's milk or soya under 6 months
  • Most outgrow it by 3–5 years
