Diarrhoea is the passage of 3 or more loose or watery stools in 24 hours (or more often than is normal for the child). Frequent passing of formed stools, or the soft stools of a breastfed baby, is not diarrhoea. Diarrhoea is a leading cause of death under 5 years, and the killer is dehydration, not the infection. Management follows IMCI and the WHO 2024 guideline on pneumonia and diarrhoea.

DysenteryVisible blood in the stool · the main dangers are damage to the gut wall, sepsis and malnutrition

Mechanisms

  • Secretory diarrhoea: toxins (cholera, ETEC) make the gut secrete salt and water · large volume · continues when fasting
  • Inflammatory (invasive) diarrhoea: organisms invade the wall (Shigella, Campylobacter, amoeba) → blood, mucus, pus cells in the stool, fever
  • Osmotic diarrhoea: unabsorbed sugar holds water in the gut (lactose after rotavirus, sorbitol) · stops when fasting · acidic stool
  • Why ORS works: glucose and sodium are absorbed together (co-transport), and water follows, even when the gut is damaged
Exam trap
  • The commonest cause of acute gastroenteritis in children is a virus (rotavirus), not bacteria; rotavirus stools are watery, not bloody.
  • The main cause of death in acute diarrhoea is dehydration.

History

  • Duration (≥ 14 days = persistent) · blood in the stool (dysentery) · number of stools and vomits · urine output (last urine) · fluids and feeds given, drugs
  • Fever, cough (other infections) · contacts, water source · vaccines (rotavirus, measles) · nutrition and growth

Investigations

  • None are needed in simple watery diarrhoea with no or some dehydration
  • Severe dehydration or IV fluids: serum sodium, potassium, urea and creatinine, blood glucose, blood gas (acidosis) · CBC
  • Stool: microscopy for blood, pus cells, amoeba, Giardia · culture in dysentery, outbreaks (cholera) or very ill children · malaria test if fever
Exam trap
  • Shock is a sign of severe dehydration; a child with only dry mouth, no tears and a sunken fontanelle has some (moderate) dehydration.
  • In SAM the skin pinch and sunken eyes are unreliable (wasting gives the same signs); diagnose dehydration from the history of watery stools and recent change in look.
  • Hypernatraemic dehydration hides: the skin feels doughy and the child looks less dry than the child really is.

The four rules of home treatment (Plan A, no dehydration)

1. Extra fluidORS after each loose stool: under 2 years 50–100 ml · 2 years or more 100–200 ml · plus breast milk, soup, rice water, clean water · give 2 packets of ORS to take home
2. Zincunder 6 months 10 mg · 6 months or more 20 mg once daily for 10–14 days · shortens the illness and prevents new episodes for 2–3 months
3. Continue feedingNever stop breastfeeding · usual food, small frequent meals · an extra meal a day for 2 weeks after
4. When to returnAt once if: not able to drink or breastfeed, becomes sicker, fever, blood in the stool, drinking poorly

Plan B (some dehydration): ORS in the health facility

  • ORS 75 ml/kg over 4 hours (give more if the child wants more) · by spoon or cup, a spoonful every 1–2 minutes; not by bottle
  • Vomiting: wait 10 minutes, then continue more slowly · continue breastfeeding · give zinc
  • Reassess after 4 hours: no dehydration → Plan A · still some → repeat Plan B and start food · severe → Plan C
  • Persistent vomiting, refusal or abdominal distension: ORS by nasogastric tube (20 ml/kg/hour) or IV

Oral rehydration solution (low-osmolarity ORS, WHO)

Sodium75 mmol/L
Glucose75 mmol/L
Chloride · potassium · citrate65 · 20 · 10 mmol/L
Total osmolarity245 mOsm/L
  • Lower osmolarity → less stool, less vomiting, fewer IV drips
  • One packet in 1 litre of clean water · use within 24 hours
  • Soft drinks, juice and sweet tea are hyperosmolar and worsen diarrhoea

Drugs: what to give and what not to give

AntibioticsNot for watery diarrhoea (WHO 2024) · give only for dysentery, suspected cholera with severe dehydration, or another infection (pneumonia, otitis)
DysenteryCiprofloxacin 15 mg/kg twice daily for 3 days · severe or young infant: ceftriaxone 50–80 mg/kg once daily for 3 days · review after 2 days
CholeraAzithromycin 20 mg/kg single dose (or doxycycline in older children) · rehydration is the life-saving treatment
Amoebiasis, giardiasisMetronidazole 10 mg/kg three times daily for 5 days (amoeba; only if seen on microscopy) · 5 mg/kg three times daily for 5 days (Giardia)
Do not giveAntidiarrhoeals (loperamide: ileus, toxic) · routine antiemetics (metoclopramide, promethazine: dystonia, drowsiness) · probiotics routinely (WHO 2024)

Complications

Electrolytes and acidHypo- or hypernatraemia · hypokalaemia (weakness, ileus, distension) · metabolic acidosis from bicarbonate loss (deep rapid breathing)
KidneyPre-renal acute kidney injury (corrects with fluids) → acute tubular necrosis if untreated · renal vein thrombosis (haematuria, flank mass)
ConvulsionsHypoglycaemia, hypo/hypernatraemia, hypocalcaemia, febrile seizure, Shigella toxin, meningitis, sinus thrombosis
Haemolytic uraemic syndromeAbout 1 week after bloody diarrhoea (E. coli O157, Shigella dysenteriae type 1): haemolytic anaemia (fragmented RBCs), low platelets, acute kidney injury
LaterSecondary lactose intolerance · persistent diarrhoea · malnutrition (vicious circle)
EPI · prevention of diarrhoea
  • Rotavirus vaccine (oral) with the pentavalent doses · measles vaccine and vitamin A.
  • Exclusive breastfeeding to 6 months · hand washing with soap · safe water · latrines · safe food.
