Fever is a core temperature ≥ 38 °C (axillary ≥ 37.5 °C). It is the commonest reason children are brought to hospital. Fever itself is a defence response and is rarely dangerous; the task is to find the cause and the child at risk of serious bacterial infection (meningitis, pneumonia, sepsis, UTI) or malaria, which in Sudan must be tested for in every febrile child.

Measuring temperature

  • Under 4 weeks: axillary electronic thermometer
  • 4 weeks – 5 years: axillary or tympanic
  • Rectal: most accurate, rarely needed
  • Hyperpyrexia: > 41 °C (suggests CNS infection, heat stroke)
  • Fever pattern is of little diagnostic value, except periodic fever (malaria, brucella) and prolonged fever

IMCI assessment of the febrile child

  1. General danger signs: unable to drink or breastfeed, vomits everything, convulsions, lethargic or unconscious → urgent referral (pre-referral antibiotic, antimalarial)
  2. Malaria test (RDT or blood film) in every febrile child in malaria areas
  3. Look for a focus: stiff neck, bulging fontanelle (meningitis) · fast breathing (pneumonia) · measles rash · ear discharge · throat · urinary symptoms · skin, joints
  4. Fever ≥ 7 days → refer for assessment (typhoid, TB, brucella, kala-azar, endocarditis)

Management of fever

  • Treat the cause · fluids, light clothing
  • Paracetamol 15 mg/kg 6-hourly (max 4 doses/day) for distress
  • Ibuprofen 5–10 mg/kg 8-hourly (avoid in dehydration, asthma attack, varicella)
  • Antipyretics do not prevent febrile seizures
  • Avoid aspirin under 16 years (Reye syndrome) and tepid sponging with cold water
  • Response to antipyretic does not tell bacterial from viral

Prolonged fever (pyrexia of unknown origin)

PUO = fever > 38 °C for more than 2 weeks (some use 8 days in children) without a cause after initial tests. In children it is usually a common disease presenting unusually, most often an infection.

GroupCauses (Sudan)
Infection (≈ 50%)Typhoid, TB, brucellosis, kala-azar, malaria (partly treated), UTI, abscess (liver, dental, sinus), osteomyelitis, infective endocarditis, HIV, EBV
Connective tissue (≈ 20%)Systemic JIA, SLE, Kawasaki, rheumatic fever, IBD
Malignancy (≈ 10%)Leukaemia, lymphoma, neuroblastoma
OthersDrug fever, factitious, central (brain injury), Kikuchi, periodic fevers

Approach to prolonged fever

History and examination
  • Contact with TB, animals and raw milk (brucella), travel, sandfly areas (Gedaref, Sennar: kala-azar)
  • Weight loss, night sweats, joint pain, rash
  • Repeat the examination daily: lymph nodes, liver, spleen, murmur, joints, fundi, teeth, sinuses
Investigations (stepwise)
  • CBC with film, ESR, CRP, blood cultures (repeated), urine culture, LFT, RFT
  • Blood film / RDT for malaria · Widal (limited) · brucella agglutination · rK39 (kala-azar) · Mantoux, CXR, HIV
  • Abdominal ultrasound · echo · bone marrow (leukaemia, kala-azar, culture)
Exam trap

Do not start blind antibiotics or steroids in prolonged fever before cultures and a bone marrow: they hide typhoid, TB and leukaemia. Fever + huge spleen + pancytopenia in a Sudanese child is kala-azar until proved otherwise.
