Fever is a core temperature ≥ 38 °C (axillary ≥ 37.5 °C). It is the commonest reason children come to hospital. Most fevers are viral and self-limiting; the task is to find the child with serious bacterial infection or malaria. Assessment follows IMCI.

Measuring temperature

  • Axillary (electronic or digital thermometer) under 4 weeks · axillary or tympanic after · rectal is not routine
  • Parents' report of fever counts · height of fever alone does not show how serious the illness is (except ≥ 39 °C at 3–6 months)

Assessment of a febrile child

Danger signs (IMCI)Not able to drink or breastfeed · vomits everything · convulsions · lethargic or unconscious → "very severe febrile disease": first dose of antibiotic and antimalarial (in malaria areas), treat low glucose, refer urgently
Look for a focusEars and throat · chest (fast breathing, chest indrawing) · neck stiffness, bulging fontanelle · rash (non-blanching = meningococcus) · abdomen, urine · joints and bones · skin infection
Red flags (high risk)Pale, mottled or blue · no response to social cues, hard to wake · grunting, RR > 60, severe chest indrawing · reduced skin turgor · non-blanching rash · bulging fontanelle, neck stiffness · status epilepticus, focal signs · age < 3 months with ≥ 38 °C
Tests for every febrile child in a malaria areaMalaria RDT or blood film · then by findings: CBC, CRP, urine dipstick and culture (especially < 3 years with no focus), blood culture, chest X-ray, LP

Fever without a focus

  • Under 1 month: always admit, full septic screen (blood, urine, LP), IV antibiotics
  • 1–3 months: full assessment, urine, blood tests · IV antibiotics if unwell, high CRP or abnormal urine
  • Older child: commonest hidden cause is UTI (test the urine), then malaria, early pneumonia, occult bacteraemia
  • Viral illness is diagnosed only when the child is well and tests are normal; review in 24–48 h

ANSWERUrinary tract infection.

Treatment of fever

  • Treat the cause; antipyretics are for discomfort, not to bring the number down
  • Paracetamol 15 mg/kg every 6 hours (max 4 doses a day) · or ibuprofen 10 mg/kg every 6–8 hours (over 3 months; not in dehydration or kidney disease)
  • Plenty of fluids, light clothing · no cold-water sponging or alcohol rubs
  • Antipyretics do not prevent febrile seizures · no aspirin (Reye syndrome)

Prolonged fever and fever of unknown origin

Prolonged fever: lasting more than 7 days · fever of unknown origin: ≥ 38 °C on most days for more than 2–3 weeks with no cause after a week of investigation. Most are common infections with an unusual course.

InfectionsTyphoid, brucellosis, TB, kala-azar, malaria, UTI, osteomyelitis, abscess (liver, dental), infective endocarditis, HIV, EBV
InflammatoryKawasaki disease (fever ≥ 5 days + red eyes, red lips, rash, swollen hands, neck node) · systemic JIA · rheumatic fever · SLE · inflammatory bowel disease
MalignancyLeukaemia, lymphoma, neuroblastoma
OthersDrug fever · factitious fever · dehydration (infants)
  • History: pattern of fever, travel, raw milk (brucella), animals, TB contact, weight loss, bone pain
  • Examination: nodes, liver and spleen, joints, eyes, skin, murmur
  • First tests: CBC and film, ESR/CRP, blood cultures (repeated), urine culture, chest X-ray, malaria film
  • Next: brucella serology, Widal (limited value), Mantoux, rK39 (kala-azar), HIV, abdominal ultrasound, echo, bone marrow
Exam trap
  • Fever with hepatosplenomegaly and pancytopenia in a child from an endemic area: think kala-azar (and leukaemia).
  • Do not give steroids or antibiotics "to see" before the diagnosis: they hide leukaemia and partially treat infections.
