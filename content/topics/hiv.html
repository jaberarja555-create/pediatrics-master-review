Most children with HIV are infected from their mother: in pregnancy, at delivery or by breastfeeding. Without treatment, half of infected infants die before age 2. With testing and ART in pregnancy, transmission falls to under 2%. Care follows the WHO consolidated HIV guidelines.

Prevention of mother-to-child transmission (PMTCT)

MotherTest every pregnant woman · lifelong ART (dolutegravir-based) from diagnosis · aim for an undetectable viral load by delivery
DeliveryAvoid prolonged rupture of membranes and unnecessary procedures · caesarean only for obstetric reasons
Infant prophylaxisLow risk (mother suppressed): nevirapine for 6 weeks · high risk (mother not on ART or high viral load): zidovudine + nevirapine for 6 weeks, nevirapine continued while breastfeeding · cotrimoxazole from 6 weeks until HIV is excluded
FeedingExclusive breastfeeding for 6 months, then complementary food, breastfeeding to 12–24 months, while the mother takes ART

Diagnosis in children

  • Under 18 months: nucleic acid test (HIV DNA/RNA PCR) at birth (where used) and 6 weeks, again at 9 months and after breastfeeding stops · antibody tests are not diagnostic (maternal antibody lasts up to 18 months)
  • 18 months and over: antibody tests (two rapid tests) as in adults
  • Test every child with TB, SAM, recurrent pneumonia, persistent diarrhoea or an HIV-positive mother
Exam trap
  • A positive HIV antibody test in a 9-month-old may be maternal antibody; a PCR is needed to diagnose infection.
  • HIV-positive mothers on ART are advised to breastfeed, not to stop.

Try it · exam-style
Why can't antibody tests diagnose HIV under 18 months?
ANSWERMaternal IgG antibody crosses the placenta and persists up to 18 months.
How should an HIV-positive mother on ART feed her baby?
ANSWERExclusive breastfeeding for 6 months, then breastfeeding with complementary foods.

Clinical features suggesting HIV

  • Failure to thrive, SAM not responding to treatment · persistent diarrhoea
  • Recurrent or severe pneumonia · Pneumocystis pneumonia (infant 2–6 months, severe hypoxia, few chest signs)
  • Oral thrush after 6 weeks, or persistent · oesophageal candida
  • Generalised lymphadenopathy, hepatosplenomegaly, parotid enlargement · finger clubbing (LIP)
  • Recurrent ear infections, TB, herpes zoster, skin rashes, developmental delay (encephalopathy)

Treatment (WHO)

WhenStart ART in every child with HIV, whatever the CD4 count, as soon as possible after diagnosis
First lineDolutegravir (DTG) + abacavir + lamivudine (dispersible DTG from 4 weeks and 3 kg) · newborns under 4 weeks: zidovudine + lamivudine + raltegravir (or nevirapine), then switch to DTG
ProphylaxisCotrimoxazole daily (PCP, toxoplasma, bacterial infections, malaria) · TB screening and isoniazid preventive therapy · all EPI vaccines (no BCG if already symptomatic)
MonitoringViral load at 6 and 12 months, then yearly (target undetectable) · growth, development, adherence counselling, disclosure
PCP treatmentHigh-dose cotrimoxazole IV or oral for 21 days + prednisolone if hypoxic
Exam trap
  • Treatment failure is most often due to poor adherence; check it before changing drugs.
  • Abacavir can cause a hypersensitivity reaction; stop and never restart it.

Name four clinical features suggesting HIV in a child.
