Acute rheumatic fever (ARF) is an autoimmune reaction 2–4 weeks after group A streptococcal sore throat: antibodies against the streptococcus cross-react with the heart, joints, brain and skin. Peak age 5–15 years; it is a disease of poverty and crowding. Only the heart is damaged permanently: rheumatic heart disease (RHD) is the commonest acquired heart disease of children in Africa. Diagnosis uses the revised Jones criteria (AHA 2015), endorsed by WHO 2024.

Major criteria (moderate–high risk populations, as in most of Africa)

CarditisAbout 50% · pancarditis · valvulitis: mitral regurgitation (apical pansystolic murmur to the axilla, the commonest), Carey Coombs mid-diastolic murmur, aortic regurgitation (early diastolic murmur) · myocarditis: tachycardia out of proportion to fever, gallop, heart failure · pericarditis: chest pain, rub, effusion · subclinical carditis (valvulitis on echo only) counts
ArthritisThe commonest (about 75%) · large joints (knee, ankle, elbow, wrist) · migratory (flitting), very painful, red, hot · dramatic response to aspirin within 48 hours · heals with no deformity · in high-risk areas monoarthritis or polyarthralgia also count as major
Sydenham choreaAbout 10%, girls · late (1–6 months after the throat infection, so ASO may be normal) · jerky purposeless movements, worse with stress, gone in sleep · milkmaid grip, spooning of hands, darting tongue · emotional lability · can stand alone as diagnosis
Erythema marginatumPink rings with clear centres on the trunk, not itchy, come and go · rare
Subcutaneous nodulesSmall, firm, painless nodules over extensor surfaces and the spine · rare · mean severe carditis

Minor criteria and diagnosis

  • Minor (moderate–high risk): fever ≥ 38 °C · monoarthralgia · ESR ≥ 30 mm/h or CRP ≥ 3 mg/dl · prolonged PR interval (unless carditis is a major)
  • Evidence of recent GAS infection (needed): raised or rising ASO or anti-DNase B · positive throat culture or rapid antigen test
  • First attack: 2 major, or 1 major + 2 minor, plus evidence of streptococcal infection
  • Recurrence: 2 major, or 1 major + 2 minor, or 3 minor, plus streptococcal evidence
  • Exceptions: chorea alone, or slowly developing (indolent) carditis, need no other criteria

Investigations

  • Echocardiography with Doppler in every suspected case (finds subclinical valvulitis) · ECG (PR interval, block) · chest X-ray (cardiomegaly)
  • ESR, CRP, CBC · ASO, anti-DNase B · throat swab · blood culture if a murmur and fever (exclude endocarditis)
Exam trap
  • Fever, raised ESR and prolonged PR are minor criteria, not major; arthralgia cannot be a minor criterion when arthritis is a major one.
  • Rheumatic fever follows streptococcal pharyngitis, not skin infection or pneumonia; post-streptococcal GN can follow both throat and skin.
  • The mitral valve is affected most; acute carditis causes regurgitation, while mitral stenosis takes years to develop.

Which manifestation of rheumatic fever leaves permanent damage?
ANSWERCarditis (valvulitis), which leads to rheumatic heart disease; arthritis and chorea heal completely.

Treatment of the acute attack

Admit, restBed rest until fever and arthritis settle; longer (weeks) with carditis and heart failure · examine the heart daily (carditis may appear later)
Eradicate GASBenzathine penicillin G IM once (600 000 units < 27 kg, 1.2 million units ≥ 27 kg) or oral penicillin V or amoxicillin for 10 days · penicillin allergy: erythromycin · this first dose starts secondary prophylaxis
Arthritis, mild carditisAspirin 50–80 mg/kg/day in 4 doses (or naproxen) until symptoms and ESR settle, usually 2–4 weeks, then taper · give after food · stop aspirin if the child gets influenza or varicella (Reye syndrome)
Moderate–severe carditis, heart failurePrednisolone 2 mg/kg/day (max 60 mg) for 2–3 weeks, then taper; start aspirin during tapering to stop a rebound · heart-failure treatment (furosemide, ACE inhibitor; digoxin cautiously) · urgent valve surgery for severe acute regurgitation
ChoreaQuiet environment · haloperidol, carbamazepine or valproate for disabling movements · penicillin prophylaxis like any ARF
  • If only arthralgia or doubtful arthritis is present, give paracetamol and observe: early aspirin can hide the migratory arthritis and the diagnosis

Prevention

  • Primary prevention: treat streptococcal sore throat within 9 days (6.1): amoxicillin or penicillin V for 10 days or one IM benzathine penicillin · better housing, less crowding
  • Secondary prevention (the most effective measure): benzathine penicillin G IM every 4 weeks (every 3 weeks in high-risk patients) · alternative: oral penicillin V twice daily or erythromycin
  • Duration:
  • No carditis: 5 years after the last attack or until age 21 (whichever is longer)
  • Carditis, no residual valve disease: 10 years or until 21
  • Residual valve disease: 10 years or until 40, sometimes for life
WHO 2024 · rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease
  • Diagnose ARF with the revised Jones criteria and use echocardiography to look for carditis.
  • Give regular benzathine penicillin G injections as secondary prophylaxis to people with ARF or RHD, including children with latent (echo-detected) RHD.

Complications and management

  • Heart failure (often triggered by recurrence of ARF, infection, anaemia or endocarditis) · pulmonary hypertension · atrial fibrillation and emboli (mitral stenosis) · infective endocarditis
  • Recurrence is suggested by fever with joint pain, a new murmur or a change in an old one, rising ESR
  • Lifelong follow-up, secondary prophylaxis, dental care · heart-failure drugs · balloon mitral valvotomy for mitral stenosis · valve repair or replacement (mechanical valve → warfarin for life, endocarditis risk)
  • Fever lasting more than a week in a child with RHD = infective endocarditis until proved otherwise (blood cultures before antibiotics)
Best next step · known RHD with fever for 2 weeks
  • Take 3 sets of blood cultures from separate sites before antibiotics and do echocardiography for vegetations (7.6).
