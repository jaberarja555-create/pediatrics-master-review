Jaundice is the commonest reason a newborn is brought to hospital. Most of it is physiological, but the history must find the baby with pathological jaundice (early onset, haemolysis, sepsis, obstruction) and the baby at risk of kernicterus, because bilirubin damage to the brain is permanent and preventable.

Background of the jaundice

Blood groupsMother and baby: Rh-negative mother (Rh disease), mother O, baby A or B (ABO incompatibility)
PregnancyFever with rash (TORCH), maternal diabetes, gestational age (preterm babies have higher levels), multiple pregnancy
DeliveryBirth weight, birth injury (cephalhaematoma, bruising), cried immediately, prolonged rupture of membranes
FeedingBreastfeeding: how often and how well; poor intake raises bilirubin (breastfeeding jaundice)
FamilyA previous baby with jaundice or exchange transfusion, G6PD deficiency, spherocytosis, consanguinity
DrugsDrugs or herbs given to the baby or taken by a breastfeeding mother with G6PD risk
Best next step
  • Investigations: total and direct serum bilirubin, blood groups of mother and baby, direct Coombs test, CBC with reticulocytes and blood film, G6PD, septic screen when unwell; TSH if prolonged.
  • Management: phototherapy by the chart for age and risk, exchange transfusion above the exchange line or with signs of kernicterus, frequent breastfeeding.

Which onset of neonatal jaundice is always pathological?
ANSWERJaundice in the first 24 hours of life.

Neonatal sepsis

Neonatal sepsis has non-specific features, so the history depends on the risk factors. Early sepsis (in the first 72 hours) comes from the mother's genital tract; late sepsis (after 72 hours) comes from the nursery or the home.

Risk factors

  • Maternal: rupture of membranes > 18 hours, fever in labour, chorioamnionitis (foul liquor), urinary infection, GBS carriage
  • Baby: prematurity, low birth weight, resuscitation at birth, male sex
  • Care: home delivery, cord care with non-sterile material, bottle feeding
  • Nursery: cannulas, ventilation and long admission (late sepsis)

Differential diagnosis and plan

Differential diagnosisHypoglycaemia, hypothermia, inborn errors of metabolism, duct-dependent heart disease, intracranial haemorrhage, HIE
InvestigationsSeptic screen: CBC (WBC < 5,000 or > 20,000/mm³, immature to total neutrophil ratio > 0.2), CRP, blood culture, LP, urine culture in late sepsis, blood glucose
TreatmentAmpicillin 50 mg/kg IV 12-hourly (first week) + gentamicin 5 mg/kg IV once daily for at least 10 days; meningitis: cefotaxime for at least 3 weeks; keep warm, feed, correct glucose

Malnutrition

The malnutrition station tests whether the student can find the cause of malnutrition (feeding, infection, social) and recognise the life-threatening conditions that kill a child with SAM in the first days. The checklist gives marks for the feeding history in detail and for managing one emergency.

History points

Presenting complaintLoss of weight, body swelling, diarrhoea, vomiting, cough, refusal of feeds; duration of each
Analysis
  • Swelling: where it started (feet), progression, relation to diet
  • Diarrhoea: frequency, consistency, blood, duration (persistent > 14 days)
  • Appetite: the appetite test decides outpatient or inpatient care
Systemic reviewCough and breathing (pneumonia, TB), convulsions, urinary symptoms, skin lesions, eye symptoms (night blindness)
Past historyRepeated diarrhoea, measles, malaria, previous admissions for malnutrition
Feeding
  • Breastfeeding: exclusive, duration, when and why stopped
  • Formula: type, dilution, bottle hygiene
  • Weaning: age, food type, preparation
  • Present diet: type, amount, frequency; feeding during diarrhoea; food taboos
Immunization and developmentMeasles vaccine (measles worsens malnutrition); delayed milestones or regression
Family and socialSimilar illness in siblings, contact with a chronic cough; parents' education, occupation and income, number of children, birth spacing, housing, water and sanitation

Life-threatening conditions in the first 24 hours

HypoglycaemiaBlood glucose < 3 mmol/L: 10% glucose 5 ml/kg IV (or 50 ml of 10% glucose orally or by NGT), then F-75 every 30 minutes for 2 hours
HypothermiaAxillary temperature < 35 °C: skin-to-skin contact with the mother, covered head, warm room, feed at once
DehydrationReSoMal 5 ml/kg every 30 minutes for 2 hours, then 5–10 ml/kg/hour; IV fluid only for shock
InfectionGive antibiotics to every child with SAM: amoxicillin, or ampicillin + gentamicin when complicated
Heart failureFrom fluid overload or anaemia: stop fluids, give furosemide; transfuse only if Hb < 4 g/dL
WHO 2023 · CMAM

The diagnosis in the station has five parts: the classification (SAM with or without oedema), the life-threatening condition present, the nutritional deficiency (vitamin A, zinc, iron), the infection present, and the predisposing factor (feeding, social).

Background

  • Feeding: breastfeeding, bottle feeding (contamination), feeding during the diarrhoea; new foods (cow's milk).
  • Contacts: others ill at home or school; source of drinking water, latrine, hand washing.
  • Immunization: rotavirus and measles vaccines.
  • Growth: weight loss, previous episodes (malnutrition is both a cause and a result of diarrhoea).
Best next step

After the history, classify the dehydration (no, some, severe) and choose Plan A, B or C (14.8). Bloody diarrhoea is treated with ciprofloxacin 15 mg/kg 12-hourly for 3 days; every child with diarrhoea also receives zinc 10 mg (under 6 months) or 20 mg daily for 10–14 days.
