Rickets is defective mineralisation of the growing bone (at the growth plate) before the epiphyses fuse; osteomalacia is the same defect in adult bone. The commonest form is nutritional rickets from vitamin D deficiency, seen in Sudan in exclusively breastfed infants kept indoors and covered, and in dark-skinned mothers with low vitamin D.

Clinical features

Skeletal
  • Head: craniotabes (ping-pong skull, earliest sign, < 6 months), frontal and parietal bossing (box-shaped head), large, late-closing AF, delayed teeth, enamel defects
  • Chest: rachitic rosary (swollen costochondral junctions), Harrison's sulcus, pigeon chest
  • Limbs: widened wrists and ankles (first sign after 6 months), bowed legs (genu varum) once walking, knock knees later, fractures
  • Spine: kyphosis, scoliosis · pelvis: narrow (later obstructed labour)
Extra-skeletal
  • Hypotonia, pot belly, delayed motor milestones, waddling gait
  • Hypocalcaemia: tetany, carpopedal spasm, convulsions, laryngospasm (young infants)
  • Recurrent chest infections (soft chest wall, ↓ immunity) · iron deficiency often coexists
  • Short stature, sweating, irritability

X-ray of the wrist (or knee)
  • Cupping and fraying of the metaphysis
  • Widened growth plate (wide gap to the epiphysis)
  • Osteopenia, thin cortex, fractures, bowing
  • Healing: a dense white line of new calcification at the metaphysis within 2–4 weeks
Exam trap

ALP is the best screening and follow-up test, but it may be normal in malnutrition (zinc-dependent enzyme) and physiologically high in growing children. Hypophosphataemic rickets has a normal calcium and normal PTH and does not respond to ordinary vitamin D.

Treatment of nutritional rickets

  1. Vitamin D (cholecalciferol), either daily: 2000 IU/day for 3 months (< 1 yr) · 3000–6000 IU/day for 3 months (1–12 yr), or stoss therapy (single large dose, when adherence is doubtful): 50 000 IU (1–3 months) · 150 000 IU (3–12 months) · 300 000 IU (1–12 yr) orally or IM
  2. Calcium for every child: 500 mg/day elemental calcium (diet or tablets), especially the first weeks, to prevent hungry bone hypocalcaemia
  3. Hypocalcaemic convulsions or tetany: 10% calcium gluconate 0.5 mL/kg (max 20 mL) IV slowly over 10 min with ECG monitoring, then oral calcium
  4. Then a maintenance dose 400 IU/day · sunlight, diet (eggs, fish, fortified milk)
  5. Follow up: ALP and X-ray after 3 months · bowing usually corrects by itself; osteotomy only for severe deformity persisting after age 4

Other types

  • Hypophosphataemic: oral phosphate 4–5 times a day + calcitriol
  • VDDR type 1: calcitriol (1,25-(OH)₂ D) lifelong
  • CKD: phosphate binders, calcitriol
  • Prevention: vitamin D 400 IU/day to all breastfed infants from birth to 1 year; sun exposure
