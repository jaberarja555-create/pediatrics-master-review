Rickets is defective mineralisation of the growing bone (growth plate and osteoid) in children; the same defect after the growth plates close is osteomalacia. The commonest cause is nutritional rickets from vitamin D deficiency, low calcium intake, or both. Treatment and prevention follow the Global Consensus on Nutritional Rickets.

Vitamin D metabolism

25(OH)D is the storage form and the test of vitamin D status · 1,25(OH)2D is the active form

Classification of rickets

Calcipenic (low calcium, PTH high)Nutritional: vitamin D deficiency and/or low calcium intake (commonest) · malabsorption (coeliac disease) · chronic liver disease · anticonvulsants (phenytoin, phenobarbitone: faster breakdown of vitamin D) · chronic kidney disease (renal osteodystrophy) · vitamin D-dependent rickets type 1 (no 1α-hydroxylase) and type 2 (receptor resistance, alopecia)
Phosphopenic (low phosphate, PTH normal)X-linked hypophosphataemic rickets (renal phosphate loss; the commonest inherited form) · Fanconi syndrome, renal tubular acidosis · rickets of prematurity

Risk factors for nutritional rickets

  • Age 6 months to 2 years (fastest bone growth)
  • Exclusive breastfeeding beyond 6 months without vitamin D supplement (breast milk is low in vitamin D)
  • Little sunlight: kept indoors, fully wrapped, covered mother · dark skin
  • Mother vitamin D deficient in pregnancy
  • Low-calcium diet: cereals rich in phytate, little milk
  • Preterm and twins (rapid growth) · a child with SAM develops rickets during catch-up growth

Clinical features

EarlyIrritability, sweating of the head · craniotabes (soft skull: the parietal or occipital bone yields like a ping-pong ball; the earliest sign, in the first year) · rachitic rosary
HeadWide, late-closing anterior fontanelle · frontal and parietal bossing (box-shaped skull) · delayed teething, enamel defects
ChestRachitic rosary: rounded, painless swelling of the costochondral junctions · Harrison sulcus (groove along the diaphragm attachment) · pigeon chest
Limbs, spineWide wrists and ankles · bow legs (genu varum) after walking, knock knees later · kyphosis · greenstick fractures · short stature
Muscles, otherHypotonia, delayed motor milestones · pot belly, constipation · chest infections · hypocalcaemic tetany or seizures (young infants)
Bow legs and pot belly
Exam trap
  • In a 4-month-old, the sign of rickets is craniotabes; bow legs appear only after the child walks.
  • Rickets gives a large, late-closing fontanelle and a large head, never a small fontanelle or microcephaly.
  • The scorbutic rosary of scurvy is sharp and tender; the rachitic rosary is rounded and painless.

  • X-ray of the wrist (or knee): widened, cupped and frayed metaphysis · wide gap to the carpal bones · thin cortex, low density
  • Healing: a white line of calcification at the metaphysis within 2–4 weeks of vitamin D (the earliest sign of response)

Over 12 years6000 IU/day300,000 IU once600 IU/day
  • Calcium with every regimen: 500 mg elemental calcium daily orally (diet or tablets)
  • Daily oral treatment is preferred; the single stoss dose is for children who may not take daily drops
  • Advise sunlight, milk and dairy foods · no weight bearing on soft, bent bones while active
  • Follow-up: ALP and wrist X-ray at 3 months · continue until both are normal, then maintenance dose
  • Most bowing corrects with growth; osteotomy only for severe deformity persisting after healing
  • Other rickets: VDDR → calcitriol + calcium · X-linked hypophosphataemic → oral phosphate + calcitriol (or burosumab) · liver or gut disease → higher vitamin D doses
Best next step · an infant with rickets has a hypocalcaemic seizure or tetany
  • Give 10% calcium gluconate 0.5 ml/kg IV (max 20 ml) over 10 minutes with cardiac monitoring.
  • Then oral calcium, and start vitamin D; giving vitamin D alone can drop the calcium further ("hungry bones").

Prevention

  • Vitamin D 400 IU daily from birth to 12 months for every infant, however fed · 600 IU daily for older children at risk
  • Vitamin D for pregnant and breastfeeding mothers · sunlight on the face and arms · calcium-rich food after 6 months
  • Preterm infants and children on anticonvulsants or with malabsorption: supplement and check 25(OH)D
Exam trap
  • Too much vitamin D causes hypercalcaemia: vomiting, constipation, polyuria, nephrocalcinosis. It is the risk of repeated large doses.
  • A healing rickets X-ray shows a dense white line at the metaphysis, not wider fraying.
