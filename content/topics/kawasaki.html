Kawasaki disease is an acute, self-limiting vasculitis of medium-sized arteries of young children, 80% of them under 5 years. Its cause is unknown; an infectious trigger in a genetically susceptible child is suspected. It matters because it attacks the coronary arteries: without treatment about 25% develop coronary aneurysms, which can cause myocardial infarction and sudden death, and it is the commonest cause of acquired heart disease in children in developed countries. Treatment with IVIG within 10 days lowers this risk to under 5%.

Diagnostic criteria

Fever for 5 days or more plus at least 4 of the 5 principal features below, with no better explanation.

Incomplete Kawasaki disease

Infants under 1 year often have fewer than 4 features yet have the highest risk of aneurysms. A child with fever ≥ 5 days and 2–3 features (or an infant with fever ≥ 7 days and no other cause) needs CRP, ESR and an echocardiogram; raised markers with supporting laboratory findings or an abnormal echo mean treatment as Kawasaki disease.

Treatment of Kawasaki disease

AHA 2017 · treatment
  • IVIG 2 g/kg as a single infusion over 10–12 hours, ideally within 10 days of the onset of fever (and also after day 10 if fever or raised markers persist)
  • Aspirin 80–100 mg/kg/day in 4 doses (anti-inflammatory) until afebrile for 48–72 hours, then 3–5 mg/kg once daily (antiplatelet) until 6–8 weeks if the echo is normal; longer when there is an aneurysm
  • Echocardiogram at diagnosis, at 1–2 weeks and at 6–8 weeks
  • Large aneurysms (≥ 8 mm): anticoagulation (warfarin or LMWH) with aspirin and long cardiology follow-up
Monitoring the response
  • First sign of improvement: fever settles within 36 hours of the end of the IVIG infusion; CRP and irritability fall over the following days
  • A rising platelet count in weeks 2–3 is the expected course, not a failure
  • IVIG resistance (10–20%): fever persisting or returning 36 hours after the infusion: give a second dose of IVIG 2 g/kg, or steroids or infliximab
Exam trap · after treatment
  • Live vaccines (measles, MMR, varicella) are delayed 11 months after IVIG, because the infused antibodies neutralise them
  • Children on aspirin who catch influenza or varicella risk Reye syndrome: give the inactivated influenza vaccine
  • Ibuprofen blocks the antiplatelet action of aspirin and is avoided
  • Steroids alone are not the treatment of Kawasaki disease

Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF)

Familial Mediterranean fever is an autosomal recessive autoinflammatory disease caused by mutations of the MEFV gene (pyrin), common in families of Arab, Turkish, Armenian and Jewish origin. The faulty pyrin lets inflammation switch on without a trigger, so the child has short, self-limiting attacks and is completely well between them. It matters because the attacks are often mistaken for an acute abdomen, and untreated FMF causes AA amyloidosis and kidney failure.

Attacks
  • Fever lasting 12–72 hours, recurring every few weeks or months, usually starting before 10 years
  • Abdominal pain from peritonitis (the commonest feature), often leading to an unnecessary appendicectomy
  • Pleuritic chest pain, monoarthritis of a large joint (knee, ankle)
  • Erysipelas-like rash: a red, hot, tender patch on the shin or foot
DiagnosisClinical (Tel Hashomer criteria), with raised CRP and serum amyloid A during attacks and normal values between them, a family history, and MEFV gene testing; a clear response to colchicine supports it
TreatmentColchicine every day for life: under 5 years ≤ 0.5 mg/day, 5–10 years 0.5–1 mg/day, over 10 years 1–1.5 mg/day; it prevents attacks and amyloidosis; side effect: diarrhoea; anti-IL-1 drugs for the few who do not respond
MonitoringFewer and milder attacks within months; CRP and serum amyloid A should be normal between attacks (if raised, increase the dose); urine protein every 6–12 months to detect amyloidosis early

PFAPA syndrome

PFAPA (periodic fever, aphthous stomatitis, pharyngitis and cervical adenitis) is the commonest periodic fever of young children (usually starting before 5 years). Fever of 3–6 days recurs like clockwork every 3–6 weeks, with mouth ulcers, sore throat and tender neck nodes; throat cultures are negative and the child grows normally. A single dose of prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg stops an attack within hours; tonsillectomy cures most children, and it fades by adolescence.

Exam trap · recurrent fever with abdominal pain

A child from an Arab family with repeated 1–3-day attacks of fever and abdominal pain, a scar of a normal appendix removed at operation, and good health between attacks has FMF. The answer is colchicine, and the complication to prevent is amyloidosis (proteinuria, nephrotic syndrome).
