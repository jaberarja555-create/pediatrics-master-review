Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive disease of the CFTR chloride channel (gene on chromosome 7; commonest mutation F508del, also called ΔF508). Chloride and water cannot leave the cells, so secretions in the lungs, pancreas, gut, liver and vas deferens become thick and sticky and block the ducts, while sweat is very salty. It is commonest in white populations but occurs in Arab and African children too. Death is usually from lung disease.

Clinical features

NewbornMeconium ileus (10–20%: bowel obstruction, bilious vomiting, distension; 3.4) · prolonged jaundice
LungsChronic wet cough, recurrent chest infections, wheeze · S. aureus early, then Pseudomonas aeruginosa (chronic, hard to clear) · bronchiectasis · clubbing · haemoptysis, pneumothorax · allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis · nasal polyps, chronic sinusitis
Pancreas (exocrine)Fat malabsorption in 85–90% → bulky, pale, oily, foul stools (steatorrhoea), big appetite, failure to thrive, low albumin (oedema), fat-soluble vitamin deficiency (A, D, E, K) · rectal prolapse
OtherCF-related diabetes (older child) · liver disease (cirrhosis) · gallstones · distal intestinal obstruction · male infertility (absent vas deferens) · salt loss in hot weather → hyponatraemic, hypochloraemic metabolic alkalosis

Diagnosis

  • Sweat test (pilocarpine iontophoresis): chloride ≥ 60 mmol/L on two occasions = CF · 30–59 = borderline (genetic test) · the test of choice
  • Newborn screening (where available): raised immunoreactive trypsinogen (IRT) on the heel-prick blood spot → sweat test
  • Genetic testing for two CFTR mutations · low faecal elastase (pancreatic insufficiency) · sputum culture
  • Chest X-ray: hyperinflation, bronchial wall thickening, ring shadows (bronchiectasis), upper lobes first

Management (team: doctor, physiotherapist, dietitian)

AirwaysChest physiotherapy daily (airway clearance) · inhaled dornase alfa (DNase) and hypertonic saline to thin mucus · bronchodilator before physiotherapy · exercise
InfectionOral flucloxacillin prophylaxis in young children · treat exacerbations early · Pseudomonas: inhaled tobramycin or colistin; IV ceftazidime + tobramycin for exacerbations · azithromycin long-term (anti-inflammatory) · annual influenza vaccine
NutritionPancreatic enzyme capsules with every meal and snack · high-calorie, high-fat diet (120–150% of normal energy) · vitamins A, D, E, K · extra salt in hot weather
New drugsCFTR modulators (elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor) correct the faulty protein in most mutations: better lung function and growth
End stageLung transplant · genetic counselling: 25% risk in each later pregnancy
Exam trap
  • The best investigation for a child with recurrent chest infections, nasal polyps and malabsorption is the sweat chloride test.
  • The typical organism in an older child with CF is Pseudomonas aeruginosa; in infants it is S. aureus.
  • Nasal polyps in a child should suggest cystic fibrosis.

Inhaled foreign body

Who and whatCommonest at 1–3 years (toddlers put things in the mouth, have no molars) · peanuts, seeds (watermelon, sunflower), small toy parts · usually lodges in the right main bronchus (wider, more vertical)
HistorySudden choking and coughing while eating or playing (often not seen) → a quiet period → later cough, wheeze, recurrent pneumonia in the same lobe
SignsLarynx: stridor, hoarse voice · bronchus: one-sided wheeze, reduced air entry on one side, not helped by salbutamol
Chest X-rayOften normal (most objects are not radio-opaque) · expiratory film: air trapping (hyperlucent lung, mediastinum shifts to the other side) · collapse or consolidation later
TreatmentRigid bronchoscopy: both diagnosis and removal · do it whenever the history is suggestive, even if the X-ray is normal
On expiration the right lung stays dark (air trapping) and the heart shifts left
Best next step · choking child (paediatric basic life support)
  • Effective cough (crying, talking): encourage coughing, watch closely; do not put fingers blindly in the mouth.
  • Ineffective cough, conscious: under 1 year: 5 back blows (head down, prone), then 5 chest thrusts · over 1 year: 5 back blows, then 5 abdominal thrusts (Heimlich) · repeat.
  • Unconscious: open the airway, remove a visible object, 5 rescue breaths, start CPR; call for help.

Bronchiectasis

  • Permanent dilatation of bronchi with pus and chronic infection
  • Causes: cystic fibrosis, severe pneumonia (measles, pertussis, TB), retained foreign body, primary ciliary dyskinesia, immunodeficiency (HIV), recurrent aspiration
  • Primary ciliary dyskinesia: wet cough from birth, chronic ear and sinus disease · Kartagener syndrome = with dextrocardia (situs inversus)
  • Features: daily wet cough with large amounts of foul sputum, worse lying down · clubbing · coarse crackles over the same area · haemoptysis · poor growth
  • Best test: high-resolution CT chest · X-ray: ring and tram-line shadows
  • Treatment: daily chest physiotherapy (postural drainage), antibiotics for exacerbations, treat the cause, vaccines · surgery for a localised lobe

Lung abscess

  • Cavity filled with pus · after aspiration (unconscious, poor swallowing), S. aureus or Klebsiella pneumonia, foreign body
  • High fever, foul sputum · X-ray: cavity with an air–fluid level · IV antibiotics (clindamycin or co-amoxiclav, cloxacillin for S. aureus) for 3–4 weeks · drainage rarely
Exam trap
  • A normal chest X-ray does not exclude an inhaled foreign body; the next step is bronchoscopy.
  • One-sided wheeze in a toddler is a foreign body until proved otherwise, not asthma.
