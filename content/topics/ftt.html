Failure to thrive (FTT) is a sign, not a diagnosis: a young child (usually under 2 years) who does not gain weight as expected. In Sudan the commonest cause is inadequate intake (undernutrition).

Definition

Low weightWeight-for-age below −2 SD (3rd centile) on repeated measurements
Falling weightWeight crossing two major centile lines downward
WastingWeight-for-length below −2 SD
Young infantUnder 6 months with no weight gain for 2–3 months
Severe wasting: visible ribs, loose skin folds on buttocks and thighs ("baggy pants")

Causes

Inadequate intake (commonest)Poverty, poor breastfeeding technique, over-diluted formula, late or poor complementary feeding · feeding difficulty: cerebral palsy, cleft palate · neglect
Losses (vomiting, malabsorption)Persistent diarrhoea, intestinal worms and giardiasis, coeliac disease, cystic fibrosis · vomiting: GORD, pyloric stenosis · urine: RTA, diabetes
Increased needsCongenital heart disease with heart failure · chronic infection: TB, HIV, kala-azar, recurrent malaria · chronic lung disease · hyperthyroidism · malignancy
Poor use of nutrientsChromosomal disorders (Down, Turner) · inborn errors of metabolism (galactosaemia) · hypothyroidism · CKD
PsychosocialNeglect, maternal depression, disturbed mother–child relationship
Exam trap
  • Breastfeeding does not cause failure to thrive (poor technique or too few feeds can).
  • The commonest cause of failure to thrive is undernutrition (not enough food), not a hidden disease.

Try it · exam-style
Most common cause of failure to thrive?
ANSWERMalnutrition (inadequate intake).
Name two causes of increased caloric needs leading to FTT.
ANSWERCongenital heart disease with heart failure; chronic infection (TB, HIV).

History and examination

  • Feeding: breast or bottle · frequency and duration · how the formula is prepared (dilution) · age and type of complementary food · who feeds the child
  • Symptoms: vomiting, diarrhoea, stool type · cough, recurrent infections · sweating on feeding · polyuria
  • Pregnancy and birth: maternal infection, IUGR, prematurity, NICU
  • Development and vaccination
  • Family: parents' heights, similar siblings, consanguinity · social: income, carers, maternal health
  • Plot weight, length and OFC and compare with earlier readings on the card
  • Signs of malnutrition (wasting, oedema) and vitamin deficiency (rickets, pallor)
  • Dysmorphic features (Down, Turner)
  • Systemic: murmur, cyanosis, chest signs, hepatosplenomegaly, thyroid
  • Signs of neglect: occipital bald patch, poor hygiene, unexplained bruises
  • Observe a feed: sucking, swallowing, mother–child interaction
Best next step
  • The diagnosis rests on history and examination.
  • Laboratory tests are chosen according to the findings, not ordered as a routine panel.

Investigations

First lineCBC · urine analysis and culture · stool for ova, parasites and reducing substances · urea, electrolytes, creatinine · LFT · TFT
Guided by cluesCoeliac screen (anti-tTG IgA) · sweat chloride · HIV test · Mantoux and chest X-ray (TB) · echo (murmur) · venous gas (RTA) · karyotype (dysmorphic) · bone age

Management

  • Treat the cause (e.g. heart failure, coeliac disease, TB)
  • Catch-up feeding: about 150 kcal/kg/day (based on expected weight) · energy-dense food, 5–6 small meals a day
  • Feeding counselling: breastfeeding support, correct formula preparation, good complementary food · meals without distraction, no force-feeding
  • Micronutrients: vitamin A, zinc, iron, multivitamins · albendazole (over 1 year) for worms
  • Follow up: weigh every 2 weeks until catch-up growth
  • Admit if:
    • SAM with complications (CMAM criteria)
    • Suspected abuse or severe neglect
    • No weight gain despite outpatient care
    • Need to observe feeding and weight gain
Sudan protocol · IMCI
  • Check every sick child for acute malnutrition (oedema, MUAC, WFH)
  • Assess feeding in children under 2 years, and in any child with anaemia or MAM
  • Counsel the mother, follow up in 14 days (MAM: 30 days)

Definition

Short statureHeight below −2 SD (3rd centile) for age and sex
Refer and assess ifHeight crossing centiles downward · height velocity below 4 cm/year before puberty · height well below the target (mid-parental) height · disproportion or dysmorphism

Causes

Normal variants (commonest)Familial (genetic) short stature · constitutional delay of growth and puberty
Undernutrition and chronic diseaseChronic malnutrition · coeliac disease, IBD · CKD, RTA · CHD · chronic lung disease (CF, severe asthma) · thalassaemia, sickle cell disease
EndocrineHypothyroidism · GH deficiency · Cushing syndrome, long-term steroids · pseudohypoparathyroidism · precocious puberty (early fusion)
SyndromesTurner, Down, Noonan, Prader–Willi, Silver–Russell
OthersIUGR without catch-up · psychosocial deprivation
DisproportionateShort limbs: achondroplasia, rickets, osteogenesis imperfecta · short trunk: mucopolysaccharidoses (Morquio), spinal disease
Exam trap
  • Marfan syndrome causes tall stature, not short stature.
  • Acute renal failure does not affect growth; only chronic kidney disease causes short stature.

Growth hormone deficiency

  • At birth: normal size · micropenis · neonatal hypoglycaemia, prolonged jaundice (with other pituitary hormones)
  • Later: proportionate short stature, velocity below 4–5 cm/year · chubby with an immature "doll" face · high-pitched voice · delayed bone age · normal intelligence
  • Causes: idiopathic (commonest) · craniopharyngioma (headache, visual field loss, calcified mass) · cranial irradiation · head injury · Laron syndrome (GH resistance, high GH, low IGF-1)
  • Diagnosis: low IGF-1 · two GH stimulation tests with a low peak · MRI of the pituitary

Assessment of a short child

  • History: birth weight and length, chronic symptoms, drugs (steroids), diet, parents' heights and age of puberty, social history
  • Examination: accurate height, plot serial readings · US/LS ratio and arm span · target height · puberty stage · dysmorphism · thyroid · fundi and visual fields · systemic examination
