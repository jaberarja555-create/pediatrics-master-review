Failure to thrive (FTT) is a sign, not a diagnosis: a young child, usually under 2 years, who does not gain weight as expected. It happens when the energy taken in is less than the energy the child needs or loses, and the commonest cause is simply inadequate intake. It matters because the first two years are the period of fastest brain growth, so prolonged undernutrition can permanently impair growth and development.

Definition

Low weightWeight-for-age below −2 SD (3rd centile) on repeated measurements
Falling weightWeight crossing two major centile lines downward
WastingWeight-for-length below −2 SD
Young infantUnder 6 months with no weight gain for 2–3 months
Severe wasting: visible ribs, loose skin folds on buttocks and thighs ("baggy pants")

Causes

Inadequate intake (commonest)
  • Too little food: poverty, poor breastfeeding technique, over-diluted formula, late or poor complementary feeding, neglect
  • Difficulty feeding: cerebral palsy, cleft palate
Losses (vomiting, malabsorption)
  • Gut: persistent diarrhoea, intestinal worms and giardiasis, coeliac disease, cystic fibrosis
  • Vomiting: GORD, pyloric stenosis
  • Urine: RTA, diabetes
Increased needs
  • Congenital heart disease with heart failure (the work of breathing uses energy)
  • Chronic infection: TB, HIV, kala-azar, recurrent malaria
  • Chronic lung disease, hyperthyroidism, malignancy
Low growth potentialChromosomal disorders (Down, Turner) · IUGR
Poor use of nutrients
  • Inborn errors of metabolism (galactosaemia)
  • Hypothyroidism
  • CKD
PsychosocialNeglect, maternal depression, disturbed mother–child relationship
Exam trap
  • Breastfeeding does not cause failure to thrive (poor technique or too few feeds can).
  • The commonest cause of failure to thrive is undernutrition (not enough food), not a hidden disease.

Try it · exam-style
Most common cause of failure to thrive?
ANSWERMalnutrition (inadequate intake).
Name two causes of increased caloric needs leading to FTT.
ANSWERCongenital heart disease with heart failure; chronic infection (TB, HIV).

History and examination

Feeding history
  • Breast or bottle; how often and how long each feed lasts
  • How the formula is prepared: over-dilution is a common cause
  • Age at starting and type of complementary food; who feeds the child
Symptoms
  • Vomiting, diarrhoea and the type of stool (malabsorption)
  • Cough and recurrent infections (TB, HIV, cystic fibrosis)
  • Sweating on feeding (heart failure); polyuria (renal or diabetes)
Background
  • Pregnancy and birth: maternal infection, IUGR, prematurity, NICU stay
  • Development and vaccination
  • Family: parents' heights, similar siblings, consanguinity
  • Social: income, carers, maternal health
Examination
  • Plot weight, length and OFC and compare with earlier readings on the card
  • Signs of malnutrition (wasting, oedema) and vitamin deficiency (rickets, pallor)
  • Dysmorphic features (Down, Turner)
  • Systemic: murmur, cyanosis, chest signs, hepatosplenomegaly, thyroid
  • Signs of neglect: occipital bald patch, poor hygiene, unexplained bruises
  • Observe a feed: sucking, swallowing, mother–child interaction
Best next step
  • The diagnosis rests on history and examination.
  • Laboratory tests are chosen according to the findings, not ordered as a routine panel.

Investigations

First line
  • CBC; urea, electrolytes, creatinine; LFT; TFT
  • Urine analysis and culture (silent UTI)
  • Stool for ova, parasites and reducing substances
Guided by clues
  • Diarrhoea: coeliac screen (anti-tTG IgA), sweat chloride
  • Infection: HIV test, Mantoux and chest X-ray (TB)
  • Murmur: echocardiography; acidosis: venous gas (RTA)
  • Dysmorphic: karyotype; short: bone age

Management

Treat the causeFor example heart failure, coeliac disease or TB
Catch-up feeding
  • About 150 kcal/kg/day (based on expected weight), compared with the normal infant need of 100–120 kcal/kg/day, because extra energy is needed to rebuild lost tissue
  • Energy-dense food in 5–6 small meals a day
Feeding counselling
  • Breastfeeding support, correct formula preparation, good complementary food
  • Meals without distraction; no force-feeding
Micronutrients and worms
  • Vitamin A, zinc, iron and multivitamins
  • Albendazole for worms (over 1 year)
Follow-up and monitoring
  • Weigh every 2 weeks until catch-up growth
  • The first sign of improvement is a better appetite, then a weight gain of 5–10 g/kg/day (faster than normal) and a curve that climbs back towards its centile
  • No gain after 2–4 weeks of good intake: look for an organic cause, or admit to observe feeding
Admit if
  • SAM with complications (CMAM criteria)
  • Suspected abuse or severe neglect
  • No weight gain despite outpatient care
  • Feeding and weight gain need to be observed
IMCI
  • Check every sick child for acute malnutrition (oedema, MUAC, WFH)
  • Assess feeding in children under 2 years, and in any child with anaemia or MAM
  • Counsel the mother on feeding and arrange follow-up; review a child with MAM in 30 days

Definition

Short statureHeight below −2 SD (3rd centile) for age and sex
Refer and assess if
  • Height crossing centiles downward
  • Height velocity below 4 cm/year before puberty
  • Height well below the target (mid-parental) height
  • Disproportion or dysmorphism

Causes

Normal variants (commonest)
  • Familial (genetic) short stature
  • Constitutional delay of growth and puberty: together about 90% of short children
Undernutrition and chronic disease
  • Chronic malnutrition
  • Gut: coeliac disease, IBD
  • Kidney: CKD, RTA; heart: CHD; lung: CF, severe asthma
  • Blood: thalassaemia, sickle cell disease
Endocrine
  • Hypothyroidism, GH deficiency
  • Cushing syndrome, long-term steroids; pseudohypoparathyroidism
  • Precocious puberty: a tall child but a short adult, because the epiphyses fuse early
SyndromesTurner, Down, Noonan, Prader–Willi, Silver–Russell
OthersIUGR without catch-up · psychosocial deprivation
Disproportionate
  • Short limbs: achondroplasia, rickets, osteogenesis imperfecta
  • Short trunk: mucopolysaccharidoses (Morquio), spinal disease
Exam trap
  • Marfan syndrome causes tall stature, not short stature.
  • Acute renal failure does not affect growth; only chronic kidney disease causes short stature.

Growth hormone deficiency

  • At birth:
    • Normal size
    • Micropenis
    • Neonatal hypoglycaemia, prolonged jaundice (with other pituitary hormones)
  • Later:
    • Proportionate short stature, velocity below 4–5 cm/year
    • Chubby with an immature "doll" face
    • High-pitched voice
    • Delayed bone age
    • Normal intelligence
  • Causes:
    • Idiopathic (commonest)
    • Craniopharyngioma (headache, visual field loss, calcified mass)
    • Cranial irradiation
    • Head injury
    • Laron syndrome (GH resistance, high GH, low IGF-1)
  • Diagnosis:
    • Low IGF-1
    • Two GH stimulation tests with a low peak (below 10 ng/mL; two tests because one can fail in a normal child)
    • MRI of the pituitary

  • History: birth weight and length, chronic symptoms, drugs (steroids), diet, parents' heights and age of puberty, social history
  • Examination:

    • Upper/lower segment ratio and arm span
    • Target height
    • Puberty stage
    • Dysmorphism
    • Thyroid
    • Fundi and visual fields
    • Systemic examination

Treatment

Treat the cause
  • Thyroxine (hypothyroidism)
  • Gluten-free diet (coeliac)
  • Nutrition
  • Treat CKD, CHD
Growth hormone
  • Indications: GH deficiency, Turner syndrome, CKD, Prader–Willi, SGA without catch-up
  • Daily subcutaneous injection until the epiphyses fuse
  • Response: height velocity should roughly double in the first year; a poor response means poor adherence, wrong dose or wrong diagnosis
Normal variants
  • Reassurance
  • Constitutional delay: short course of testosterone (boys) or oestrogen (girls) if puberty is very late
