Neonatal sepsis is a systemic bacterial infection in the first 28 days and a leading cause of neonatal death. Signs are non-specific, so every sick newborn is treated as septic until proved otherwise. Treatment follows the WHO 2024 recommendations for young infants.

Early-onset compared with late-onset sepsis

Early-onset (first 72 h)Late-onset (after 72 h)
SourceMother: birth canal, ascending infectionHospital (lines, tubes, hands) or community
Risk factorsProlonged rupture of membranes > 18 h, maternal fever or chorioamnionitis, maternal GBS or UTI, preterm labourPrematurity, long admission, IV lines, ventilation, poor hand hygiene, poor cord care
OrganismsGroup B streptococcus, E. coli, Klebsiella, ListeriaStaph. aureus, coagulase-negative staph, Klebsiella, Pseudomonas, E. coli, Candida
Main picturePneumonia, respiratory distress, shockBacteraemia with meningitis, osteomyelitis, UTI

Clinical features

  • Poor feeding, lethargy, irritability, weak Moro and suck
  • Temperature instability: fever or hypothermia (commoner in preterm)
  • Respiratory distress, apnoea, cyanosis · mottled skin, slow capillary refill
  • Vomiting, distension · jaundice · hypo- or hyperglycaemia
  • Meningitis: bulging fontanelle, seizures, high-pitched cry (neck stiffness is rare)
  • Late: petechiae, bleeding (DIC), sclerema (hard skin, bad sign), shock

Investigations (septic screen) and treatment

Septic screenBlood culture (before antibiotics) · CBC (WBC < 5000 or > 20,000, low platelets) · CRP · lumbar puncture in every baby with suspected sepsis who is stable enough · urine culture after 72 h · chest X-ray if distress · glucose
First choiceAmpicillin 50 mg/kg IV/IM (12-hourly in week 1, 8-hourly after) + gentamicin 5 mg/kg once daily (7.5 mg/kg after week 1), for at least 10 days (WHO 2024)
MeningitisCefotaxime (or ceftriaxone after the first week, not in jaundice) + ampicillin (Listeria) or gentamicin, for at least 3 weeks
Late-onset, skin or umbilical sourceCloxacillin (or flucloxacillin) + gentamicin · resistant hospital infection: by culture (meropenem, vancomycin)
SupportiveWarmth, oxygen, IV fluids and glucose, treat shock (10 ml/kg saline), breast milk by NGT, monitor glucose and jaundice
PreventionClean delivery, hand washing, cord care, exclusive breastfeeding · intrapartum penicillin for GBS-positive or high-risk mothers

Name four risk factors for early-onset sepsis.
ANSWERMembranes ruptured > 18 h, maternal fever or chorioamnionitis, GBS colonisation, prematurity.

Features common to congenital infections

IUGR · microcephaly, seizures · hepatosplenomegaly, jaundice (often conjugated) · thrombocytopenia, petechiae, "blueberry muffin" rash · chorioretinitis, cataract · sensorineural deafness · developmental delay. Diagnosis: specific IgM in the baby, PCR (urine, blood, CSF), cranial imaging.

The main congenital infections

ToxoplasmosisCat faeces, undercooked meat · triad: hydrocephalus, chorioretinitis, diffuse intracranial calcification · treatment: pyrimethamine + sulfadiazine (+ folinic acid) for 1 year
RubellaWorst in the first trimester · triad: cataract, sensorineural deafness, congenital heart disease (PDA, pulmonary artery stenosis) · blueberry muffin rash · prevented by vaccinating girls before pregnancy
CytomegalovirusThe commonest congenital infection · 90% silent at birth · periventricular calcification, microcephaly, petechiae · commonest infectious cause of sensorineural deafness · oral valganciclovir for 6 months if symptomatic
Herpes simplexCaught during delivery from genital herpes · days 5–21: skin, eye and mouth vesicles, encephalitis or disseminated disease · IV aciclovir · caesarean section if the mother has active lesions
SyphilisEarly: snuffles (bloody nasal discharge), rash on palms and soles, hepatosplenomegaly, osteochondritis (pseudoparalysis) · late: Hutchinson teeth, saddle nose, sabre shins, interstitial keratitis · benzylpenicillin for 10 days · screen every pregnant woman
VaricellaBefore 20 weeks: limb hypoplasia and zig-zag skin scars · maternal chickenpox 5 days before to 2 days after delivery → severe neonatal varicella: give VZIG, aciclovir if ill
Hepatitis B, HIVHepatitis B: vaccine within 24 h + HBIG if the mother is HBsAg positive · HIV: maternal ART, infant nevirapine prophylaxis, exclusive breastfeeding on ART
Exam trap
  • Calcification around the ventricles = CMV · scattered (diffuse) calcification with hydrocephalus = toxoplasmosis.
  • Rubella risk is highest in early pregnancy; toxoplasma transmission rises with gestation, but early infection is more severe.

Local infections

Gonococcal conjunctivitisDays 2–5: severe purulent discharge, swollen lids · can perforate the cornea · ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg IM once (max 125 mg) + frequent eye washing · treat the parents
Chlamydial conjunctivitisDays 5–14: milder discharge · may cause pneumonia at 1–3 months · oral erythromycin for 14 days (or azithromycin 3 days)
OmphalitisRedness spreading from the cord to the skin, pus, foul smell · cloxacillin + gentamicin IV · a red stump with pus only: clean and local antiseptic
Skin pustulesFew: local antiseptic · many (10 or more) or with sick baby: systemic antibiotics (Staph. aureus)
Oral thrushWhite plaques that do not wipe off · nystatin drops · treat the mother's nipples

Neonatal tetanus

  • Clostridium tetani spores enter the umbilical stump (unclean cutting or dressing of the cord) in a baby whose mother was not vaccinated
  • Day 3–14: a baby who sucked well stops sucking (trismus), then generalised spasms on touch or noise, opisthotonus, clenched fists · baby is conscious, no fever at first
  • Death from laryngeal spasm, apnoea, aspiration pneumonia, sepsis
  • Quiet, dark room, minimal handling · feed expressed breast milk by NGT · IV fluids
  • Tetanus immunoglobulin 500 IU IM once (or antitoxin) · metronidazole (or benzylpenicillin) for 7–10 days · diazepam for spasms · clean the cord
  • Prevention: Td vaccine to every woman of child-bearing age and in pregnancy, clean delivery and clean cord care
Best next step · a 7-day-old home-delivered baby stops sucking and has spasms when touched
  • Diagnose neonatal tetanus: admit to a quiet room, give tetanus immunoglobulin, metronidazole and diazepam, and feed by NGT.
  • Give the mother Td vaccine and report the case to the EPI.
