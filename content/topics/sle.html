Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disease in which autoantibodies and immune complexes deposit in small vessels of the skin, joints, kidneys, blood and brain. In children it starts mostly in adolescent girls (girls 5 : 1 before puberty and 9 : 1 after), and it is more severe than in adults, with more kidney and nervous system disease. It matters because lupus nephritis is the main cause of death and kidney failure, while early treatment keeps most children well.

Investigations

ANAPositive in over 95%: the best screening test; a negative ANA almost excludes SLE, but a positive one is not specific (it is found in healthy children)
Anti-dsDNASpecific, and its level rises with disease activity, especially nephritis: used to follow the disease
Anti-SmThe most specific antibody, but found in fewer patients
Complement C3 and C4Low in active disease, because immune complexes consume them; they rise again with treatment
ESR and CRPESR high with a normal CRP is typical; a high CRP suggests infection or serositis
OthersCBC and Coombs test, urinalysis and urine protein : creatinine ratio, renal function, antiphospholipid antibodies, anti-histone (drug-induced lupus)

Treatment of SLE

All patients
  • Hydroxychloroquine 5 mg/kg/day (maximum 400 mg): reduces flares and protects the kidneys; yearly eye examination for retinal toxicity
  • Sun protection, vaccines (no live vaccines on immunosuppression), blood pressure control, calcium and vitamin D
Mild disease (skin, joints)NSAIDs for arthritis, low-dose prednisolone for a short time
Moderate diseasePrednisolone 1–2 mg/kg/day (maximum 60 mg), tapered, with a steroid-sparing drug: azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil or methotrexate
Severe disease (nephritis class III–IV, CNS lupus)IV methylprednisolone 30 mg/kg/day (maximum 1 g) for 3 days, then oral steroid with mycophenolate mofetil or IV cyclophosphamide; rituximab when these fail
Monitoring the response
  • First signs of improvement: fever and rash settle and joints ease within days of steroids; then C3 and C4 rise and anti-dsDNA falls
  • Kidney targets: falling proteinuria (urine protein : creatinine ratio) and normal creatinine and blood pressure; remission of nephritis takes months
  • Failure or flare: new proteinuria, falling complement, rising anti-dsDNA or new cytopenias means stepping up treatment; fever with a high CRP means infection until proven otherwise

Neonatal lupus and drug-induced lupus

Neonatal lupus
  • Caused by maternal anti-Ro (SSA) and anti-La (SSB) antibodies crossing the placenta; the mother may have no symptoms
  • Congenital complete heart block: permanent, may need a pacemaker
  • Annular rash around the eyes, cytopenias and hepatitis: transient, gone by 6 months as the antibodies clear
Drug-induced lupusCaused by drugs such as hydralazine, isoniazid, procainamide, anticonvulsants and minocycline; anti-histone antibodies positive; the kidneys and brain are usually spared; it resolves when the drug is stopped

Juvenile dermatomyositis (JDM)

Juvenile dermatomyositis is an autoimmune vasculopathy of small vessels of skin and muscle, and the commonest inflammatory myopathy of childhood. It affects children 4–10 years (girls more often). Unlike the adult disease, it is not linked to cancer.

MuscleSymmetrical proximal weakness: difficulty climbing stairs, combing hair and rising from the floor (Gowers sign); muscles may be tender; weakness of the swallowing and breathing muscles is dangerous (aspiration, respiratory failure)
Skin
  • Heliotrope rash: violet discoloration and swelling of the upper eyelids
  • Gottron papules: red scaly papules over the knuckles, elbows and knees
  • Nail-fold capillary changes, photosensitive rash of the face and shoulders (shawl sign)
  • Calcinosis of skin and muscle (late, when treatment is delayed)
InvestigationsRaised muscle enzymes (CK, LDH, AST, aldolase); MRI of the thighs shows muscle inflammation; EMG and muscle biopsy when the picture is not typical
TreatmentIV methylprednisolone then oral prednisolone, with methotrexate from the start; IVIG for resistant disease; hydroxychloroquine and sun protection for the skin; physiotherapy
MonitoringMuscle strength and swallowing improve over weeks; CK falls first; a return of weakness or rising CK signals a flare
Exam trap · dermatomyositis versus Duchenne muscular dystrophy

Both cause proximal weakness and a positive Gowers sign with a high CK. Dermatomyositis has a rash, tender muscles, raised inflammatory markers and responds to steroids; Duchenne affects boys from early childhood with calf pseudohypertrophy and no rash (see 11.5).
