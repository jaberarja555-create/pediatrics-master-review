The abdominal station is most often a child with hepatosplenomegaly, ascites or an abdominal mass. The child must lie flat and relaxed; palpation starts away from any tender area and from the right iliac fossa, so that a large liver or spleen is not missed by starting too high.

Steps of the examination

PositionChild lying flat with arms by the sides (infant on the mother's lap); expose from the nipples to the knees, keeping the genitalia covered until needed
GeneralPallor, jaundice, wasting, oedema, lymph nodes, signs of chronic liver disease (spider naevi, palmar erythema)
InspectionContour (distension, pot belly), umbilicus (hernia), dilated veins, scars, traditional cautery marks (they point to a chronic illness the family treated), visible peristalsis (pyloric stenosis)
Superficial palpationWatch the child's face; tenderness, guarding, temperature, superficial masses
Deep palpationStart in the right iliac fossa and move up for the liver and diagonally for the spleen; then the kidneys bimanually (ballotable)
PercussionLiver span (upper border), spleen; shifting dullness or fluid thrill for ascites
AuscultationBowel sounds; bruit over the liver or kidneys
FinishHernial orifices and genitalia (undescended testis, hydrocele); offer the anal examination; cover, thank, summarise

Reporting an organ

  • Liver: size below the costal margin in the mid-clavicular line (cm), span by percussion, edge (sharp or rounded), consistency, surface, tenderness, bruit
  • Normal: the edge may be felt up to 2 cm below the margin in infants
  • Spleen: size in cm from the left costal margin towards the right iliac fossa, consistency, tenderness, notch
  • A palpable spleen is enlarged (except the tip in some infants)

Where does palpation for the spleen start?
ANSWERIn the right iliac fossa, moving towards the left costal margin.
How is ascites confirmed at the bedside?
ANSWERBy shifting dullness (or a fluid thrill in tense ascites).

Hepatosplenomegaly

Hepatosplenomegaly is a common long-case and OSCE finding. The examiner expects the student to describe the organs correctly and then give a differential diagnosis that starts with the infections common in the area, because a big spleen in a child from an endemic region is usually malaria, kala-azar or schistosomiasis.

Causes

InfectionsMalaria (hyper-reactive malarial splenomegaly), kala-azar (huge spleen, fever, wasting), schistosomiasis (portal hypertension: firm spleen, small liver), typhoid, brucellosis, TB, HIV, infectious mononucleosis, endocarditis
HaematologicalThalassaemia, sickle cell disease (young child, before auto-infarction), hereditary spherocytosis, leukaemia, lymphoma
CongestiveHeart failure (liver more than spleen), portal hypertension, Budd–Chiari syndrome
Storage and metabolicGaucher disease (spleen), glycogen storage disease (liver), Niemann–Pick disease

Clues on examination

  • Fever and wasting with a huge spleen: kala-azar; ask about residence (Gedaref, Sennar, Blue Nile).
  • Pallor and jaundice with frontal bossing: haemolytic anaemia (thalassaemia, sickle cell disease).
  • Lymph nodes, bruising and bone pain: leukaemia or lymphoma.
  • Haematemesis, firm spleen, dilated abdominal veins: portal hypertension (schistosomiasis).
Best next step
  • Investigations: CBC with blood film (malaria parasites, blasts, sickle cells, target cells), reticulocytes, liver function, rK39 test or bone marrow or splenic aspirate for kala-azar, Hb electrophoresis, abdominal ultrasound with portal vein Doppler, stool and urine for schistosoma eggs.
