Every examination station starts the same way: greet, wash hands, take permission, position and expose the child appropriately, and look at the child as a whole before touching. A child is examined opportunistically: observe while the child sits on the mother's lap, warm your hands, and leave unpleasant parts (throat, ears, temperature) to the end.

WeightUndressed infant on a calibrated scale, zeroed first; older child in light clothes, standing still
Length (under 2 years)Lying on a length board: two people, head against the fixed end, knees straight, feet flat against the moving foot piece
Height (2 years and over)Standing without shoes, heels, buttocks and shoulders against the board, looking straight ahead

Plotting and reading the chart

  1. Choose the right WHO chart for the sex and the measurement.
  2. Work out the exact age; use the corrected age for a preterm baby until 2 years.
  3. Plot each point as a small dot and join serial points.
  4. State the centile or Z-score and the trend: following a line, crossing centiles downwards, or flat.
  5. Compare weight, height and head circumference with each other, then interpret and give the next step.

Definition and approach

Short stature is a height below −2 SD (the 3rd centile) for age and sex. Most short children are normal (familial short stature or constitutional delay); the station tests whether the student can separate them from the child with a disease, using the growth pattern, the parents' heights and the bone age.

History and examination

  • Birth: birth weight (intrauterine growth restriction), gestation
  • Diet and chronic illness: diarrhoea (coeliac disease), asthma, renal or heart disease, sickle cell disease
  • Drugs: long-term steroids
  • Family: parents' heights, timing of the parents' puberty
  • Social: neglect, poverty
  • Measure height, weight, arm span and sitting height (body proportions)
  • Plot the height and the mid-parental height
  • Look for dysmorphism (Turner syndrome in a girl: neck webbing, wide-spaced nipples), goitre, signs of chronic disease
  • Pubertal stage

How to assess

  • Greet the mother, put the child on her lap and use simple toys: cubes, a rattle, a ball, a crayon, a picture book.
  • Assess each field in turn: gross motor → fine motor and vision → hearing, speech and language → social.
  • Give a developmental age for each field, then compare it with the chronological (or corrected) age.
  • Conclude: normal, specific delay (one field) or global delay (two or more fields); suggest the cause and the plan (hearing test, TSH, referral to physiotherapy).

Exam trap
  • Not walking by 18 months, no words by 18 months, and no social smile by 8 weeks are red flags that need assessment.
  • Hand preference before 12 months is abnormal: it suggests weakness of the other side (hemiplegic cerebral palsy).
  • Loss of skills already learned is always abnormal and suggests a degenerative disease.
