Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA) is arthritis of unknown cause that begins before 16 years and lasts at least 6 weeks, once other causes have been excluded. It is the commonest chronic rheumatic disease of childhood. It results from an autoimmune attack on the synovium in a genetically susceptible child, and it matters because untreated synovitis erodes the joint, disturbs growth and can cause silent blindness from uveitis.

Diagnosis of arthritis

A joint has arthritis when it is swollen (effusion), or when two of the following are present: limited movement, pain or tenderness on movement, and warmth. Young children rarely complain of pain; they present with a limp, morning stiffness or reluctance to use a limb.

Exam trap · systemic JIA is a diagnosis of exclusion

Fever for weeks with rash, lymph nodes and a large liver and spleen is also the picture of leukaemia, kala-azar, TB and SLE. Before calling it systemic JIA, look at the blood film (and bone marrow if any doubt), because steroids given for presumed JIA can mask leukaemia.

Investigations

JIA is a clinical diagnosis: no blood test proves it. Tests are used to exclude other causes, to classify the subtype and to predict the outcome.

To exclude other causes
  • CBC and blood film (leukaemia), bone marrow when in doubt
  • Joint aspiration when one joint is hot (septic arthritis), ASO titre and echocardiogram (rheumatic fever), brucella serology, tuberculin test
  • X-ray or ultrasound of the joint
InflammationESR and CRP raised, anaemia of chronic disease, high platelets (most marked in systemic JIA, with very high ferritin)
Classification and prognosis
  • ANA: predicts uveitis risk
  • RF and anti-CCP: positive in the polyarticular form with the worst outcome
  • HLA-B27: enthesitis-related arthritis
  • A negative ANA or RF does not exclude JIA

Complications

  • Chronic anterior uveitis (iridocyclitis): painless and silent, so it causes cataract, glaucoma and blindness unless found by slit-lamp screening, every 3 months in ANA-positive oligoarticular disease starting before 7 years
  • Local growth disturbance: the inflamed knee grows faster, giving leg-length discrepancy; jaw involvement gives micrognathia
  • Joint contractures and erosions
  • Growth failure and delayed puberty (disease activity and steroids)
  • Osteoporosis
  • Macrophage activation syndrome in systemic JIA (below)
  • Amyloidosis after long uncontrolled disease (nephrotic syndrome)
Macrophage activation syndrome (MAS)

A life-threatening flare of systemic JIA in which activated macrophages consume blood cells. Suspect it when a child with systemic JIA develops persistent fever with a falling ESR (fibrinogen is used up), falling platelets and white cells, very high ferritin, high triglycerides, abnormal liver tests and bleeding. Treat urgently with IV methylprednisolone and ciclosporin or anakinra.

Treatment

Care is multidisciplinary: paediatric rheumatologist, physiotherapist, ophthalmologist and the family. The aim is inactive disease: no swollen joints, normal ESR and CRP, and normal growth.

NSAIDs (first step)For pain and stiffness while the diagnosis is confirmed: ibuprofen 10 mg/kg 6–8-hourly or naproxen 5–10 mg/kg 12-hourly; they do not prevent joint damage
Intra-articular steroidTriamcinolone hexacetonide into the swollen joint: the treatment of choice in oligoarticular JIA
Methotrexate (first-line DMARD)10–15 mg/m² once a week orally or subcutaneously, with folic acid; for polyarticular disease or oligoarticular disease that keeps relapsing; check CBC and liver tests every 1–3 months
Biological agentsAnti-TNF (etanercept, adalimumab) when methotrexate fails; anti-IL-6 (tocilizumab) and anti-IL-1 (anakinra) for systemic JIA
Systemic steroidsOnly for systemic JIA with serositis or MAS, or as a short bridge in severe polyarthritis, because long use stunts growth and thins bone
SupportivePhysiotherapy and splints to keep movement, eye screening, growth and nutrition, calcium and vitamin D, school support
Monitoring the response
  • First signs of improvement: shorter morning stiffness, fewer swollen joints, falling ESR and CRP; with methotrexate expect benefit after 6–12 weeks
  • Target: inactive disease within about 6 months, normal growth, no uveitis
  • Failure: active joints after 3 months of adequate methotrexate means stepping up to a biological agent; methotrexate is stopped about a year after remission
