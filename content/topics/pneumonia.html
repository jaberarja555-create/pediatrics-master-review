Pneumonia is infection of the lung tissue (alveoli). It is the leading infectious cause of death in children under 5 years. The most useful sign is fast breathing; the sign of severity is chest indrawing and, above all, the general danger signs. Management follows IMCI and the WHO 2024 guideline on pneumonia and diarrhoea.

Causes by age

NewbornGroup B streptococcus, E. coli and other Gram-negative bacilli, Listeria (3.6)
1–3 monthsViruses (RSV) · Chlamydia trachomatis (afebrile, staccato cough, conjunctivitis) · pertussis · S. pneumoniae
3 months–5 yearsViruses are commonest (RSV, parainfluenza, influenza, adenovirus) · commonest bacteria: Streptococcus pneumoniae, then H. influenzae type b, S. aureus
5 years and overS. pneumoniae (commonest bacterium) · Mycoplasma pneumoniae (atypical) · Chlamydophila pneumoniae · TB
Special groupsSAM and HIV: S. aureus, Gram-negatives, Pneumocystis jirovecii (HIV infant under 1 year, 4.6), TB · after measles: S. aureus, adenovirus · sickle cell: pneumococcus (acute chest syndrome)

Clinical features

  • Cough and fever (fever may be absent in young infants and in SAM)
  • Fast breathing (the most sensitive sign), chest indrawing, nasal flaring, grunting (severe), head nodding, cyanosis
  • Lower-lobe pneumonia → abdominal pain (mimics appendicitis) · upper-lobe pneumonia → neck stiffness (mimics meningitis)
  • Lobar pneumonia (consolidation): dull percussion, bronchial breathing, increased vocal resonance, fine crackles
  • Bronchopneumonia (commoner in infants): scattered crackles on both sides, percussion often normal
  • Wheeze suggests a viral or atypical cause

Classification and treatment of cough or difficult breathing (2–59 months)

Count the breaths for a full minute while the child is calm. Fast breathing: ≥ 50/min at 2–11 months, ≥ 40/min at 12 months–5 years (≥ 60/min under 2 months).

WHO 2024 · pneumonia in children 2–59 months
  • Chest indrawing pneumonia without danger signs is treated with oral amoxicillin for 5 days at home, not with injections in hospital.
  • Fast-breathing pneumonia (no chest indrawing): oral amoxicillin for 3 days in areas of low HIV prevalence (5 days otherwise).
  • A child with wheeze and fast breathing first gets a rapid-acting bronchodilator (salbutamol) and is reassessed before being classified.
Exam trap
  • The single best sign of pneumonia in a young child is fast breathing, not crackles.
  • In current WHO practice, chest indrawing alone is "pneumonia" (oral amoxicillin at home); the child is "severe" only with a danger sign, cyanosis or SpO2 < 90%.

Investigations

Pulse oximetryIn every child with pneumonia: the most useful test for deciding on oxygen and admission
Chest X-rayNot needed for simple pneumonia · do it if severe, not improving, or a complication is suspected · lobar consolidation (pneumococcus) · patchy bilateral shadows (bronchopneumonia) · perihilar streaky shadows with hyperinflation (viral, mycoplasma) · pneumatoceles, effusion (S. aureus)
Blood testsCBC (high neutrophils in bacterial, lymphocytes in viral), CRP · blood culture (positive in only 10–20%) · glucose in very sick children · malaria test in fever · HIV test
OthersPleural tap if effusion · gastric aspirate or sputum GeneXpert for TB (4.5) · nasal swab PCR for viruses

Hospital treatment of severe pneumonia

  1. Oxygen by nasal prongs when SpO2 < 90% (or central cyanosis, grunting, inability to drink): aim for 90% or more
  2. Ampicillin 50 mg/kg IV or IM every 6 hours (or benzylpenicillin 50 000 units/kg) plus gentamicin 7.5 mg/kg once daily, for at least 5 days · when better, change to oral amoxicillin
  3. No improvement in 48 hours or meningitis possible: ceftriaxone 80 mg/kg once daily
  4. Suspected S. aureus (pneumatoceles, empyema, skin boils, after measles, rapid worsening): cloxacillin 50 mg/kg every 6 hours + gentamicin, for 3 weeks
  5. Paracetamol for fever · feed (nasogastric tube if needed) · IV fluids only if the child cannot drink, at 70–80% of maintenance (risk of SIADH) · salbutamol if wheeze
  6. Severe pneumonia in an HIV-exposed infant: add high-dose cotrimoxazole for Pneumocystis pneumonia (4.6)

Atypical (mycoplasma) pneumonia

  • School-age child (5–15 years), gradual onset · headache, sore throat, low fever · persistent dry cough · few chest signs, sometimes wheeze
  • "Walking pneumonia": the X-ray looks worse than the child
  • Diagnosis: clinical · CBC usually normal · cold agglutinins in about half · PCR
  • Extrapulmonary features: erythema multiforme, Stevens–Johnson syndrome, haemolytic anaemia, encephalitis, Guillain–Barré syndrome, arthritis, myocarditis
  • Treatment: a macrolide: azithromycin 10 mg/kg once daily for 3 days (or erythromycin, clarithromycin) · penicillins do not work (no cell wall)
Exam trap
  • Viral pneumonia gives perihilar streaky shadows and hyperinflation; large effusion, lobar consolidation, abscess and pneumatoceles point to bacteria.
  • Mycoplasma has no cell wall, so it does not respond to amoxicillin or cephalosporins: give a macrolide.

Parapneumonic effusion and empyema

Suspect whenPneumonia with fever persisting beyond 48 hours of good antibiotics, pleuritic chest pain, stony dull percussion, absent breath sounds
CausesS. pneumoniae (commonest) · S. aureus · H. influenzae · TB (lymphocytic effusion, 4.5)
ImagingX-ray: homogeneous opacity, meniscus rising to the axilla, lost costophrenic angle · ultrasound: confirms fluid, shows loculations, guides the tap
Empyema meansPus in the pleura, or fluid with organisms on Gram stain, pH < 7.2, low glucose, high LDH
TreatmentIV antibiotics (ceftriaxone + cloxacillin) for 3–4 weeks in total · chest drain for empyema or a large effusion with distress · loculated pus: fibrinolytics through the drain or VATS (thoracoscopic surgery) · most children recover fully

Other complications

  • Lung abscess: cavity with an air–fluid level · aspiration, S. aureus · long antibiotic course
  • Pneumatoceles: thin-walled air cysts (S. aureus) · most disappear · a tension pneumatocele or pyopneumothorax needs a drain
  • Systemic: septicaemia, meningitis, heart failure, SIADH (low sodium), paralytic ileus

Recurrent or non-resolving pneumonia

  • Same lobe every time → a local cause: inhaled foreign body, enlarged TB node pressing on a bronchus, congenital lung malformation, bronchiectasis
  • Different lobes → a general cause: aspiration (GOR, cerebral palsy, H-type tracheo-oesophageal fistula), cystic fibrosis, immunodeficiency (HIV), sickle cell disease, primary ciliary dyskinesia, CHD with a left-to-right shunt
  • Not improving: wrong drug or dose, resistant organism, empyema or abscess, TB, foreign body, HIV, malnutrition, a wrong diagnosis (heart failure)
  • Work-up: chest X-ray, HIV test, sweat test, TB tests, immunoglobulins, bronchoscopy, CT chest
Best next step · pneumonia still febrile after 48 hours
  • Re-examine and repeat the chest X-ray (with ultrasound) to look for effusion or empyema; tap the fluid and change to ceftriaxone ± cloxacillin.
