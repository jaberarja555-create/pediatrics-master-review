Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency: untreated it kills, and survivors may be left deaf or brain-damaged. Sudan lies in the African meningitis belt with epidemics in the dry season. Treatment follows the WHO 2025 meningitis guidelines.

Causes by age

Newborn (< 1 month)Group B streptococcus, E. coli, Listeria (see 3.6)
1 month – 5 yearsPneumococcus, meningococcus, Hib (rare after vaccination)
Older childMeningococcus (epidemics: serogroups A, C, W, X) and pneumococcus
OthersTB meningitis (subacute) · viral (enteroviruses, mumps) · cryptococcus in HIV

Clinical features

  • Infants: fever or hypothermia, poor feeding, vomiting, irritability, high-pitched cry, bulging fontanelle, seizures · neck stiffness often absent
  • Older children: fever, headache, vomiting, photophobia, neck stiffness, Kernig and Brudzinski signs, confusion
  • Meningococcal septicaemia: non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash (glass test), shock, cold hands, may occur without meningitis
  • Raised ICP: falling consciousness, bradycardia with hypertension, unequal pupils, papilloedema (late)

Normal CSF: clear, ≤ 5 lymphocytes, protein 0.15–0.4 g/L, glucose about 2/3 of blood glucose (take a blood glucose at the same time). Send Gram stain, culture, latex or PCR; blood culture as well.

Best next step · a drowsy child with signs of raised ICP or a purpuric rash
  • Do not delay antibiotics: take a blood culture and give ceftriaxone at once; the LP waits.
  • Delay LP when there is reduced consciousness (GCS < 9) or falling GCS, focal signs, papilloedema, shock, a bleeding disorder or skin infection at the site.

ANSWERReduced or falling consciousness, focal signs or papilloedema, shock, bleeding disorder (or local skin infection).
Why is neck stiffness unreliable in infants?
ANSWERIt is often absent; look for a bulging fontanelle, irritability and poor feeding.

Treatment of bacterial meningitis

AntibioticCeftriaxone 100 mg/kg/day IV (once daily or 12-hourly, max 4 g) or cefotaxime, as soon as possible · add ampicillin under 3 months (Listeria) · add vancomycin where penicillin-resistant pneumococcus is common
DurationMeningococcus 5–7 days · Hib 7–10 days · pneumococcus 10–14 days · unknown cause 10 days · newborn 3 weeks · in epidemics WHO uses ceftriaxone for 5 days
Dexamethasone0.15 mg/kg IV 6-hourly for 4 days, started with or before the first antibiotic dose (non-epidemic setting) · reduces deafness · not in newborns
SupportiveABC, treat shock (fluid boluses) · head up 30° · treat seizures · check glucose and sodium (SIADH) · normal maintenance fluids once perfusion is restored · feed early
ContactsMeningococcus: close contacts get ciprofloxacin single dose (or rifampicin 2 days, or ceftriaxone IM once) · vaccinate in outbreaks

Complications

  • Acute: shock and DIC (meningococcus), seizures, raised ICP, SIADH (hyponatraemia), subdural effusion (persistent fever, bulging fontanelle), brain abscess
  • Late: sensorineural deafness (the commonest; hearing test for every survivor), hydrocephalus, epilepsy, cerebral palsy, learning difficulty, limb loss after purpura fulminans

Prevention

Vaccines: Hib and pneumococcal conjugate (in the pentavalent and PCV of the EPI) · meningococcal A conjugate (MenAfriVac) and multivalent vaccines in the meningitis belt.

Encephalitis

  • Inflammation of the brain: fever + altered behaviour or consciousness, seizures, focal signs
  • Causes: herpes simplex (the most important to treat), enteroviruses, measles (acute or SSPE years later), mumps, arboviruses
  • CSF: lymphocytes, normal glucose · HSV PCR · MRI (temporal lobe) · EEG
  • Start aciclovir 20 mg/kg IV 8-hourly at once in any suspected encephalitis, for 21 days if HSV is confirmed
