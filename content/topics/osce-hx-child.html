A child with cough is asked three groups of questions: the cough itself (duration, type, timing), the associated symptoms that point to the cause (fever, wheeze, weight loss), and the background that makes a cause likely (atopy, TB contact, smoking at home). In a known asthmatic the station also asks about control and treatment.

The known asthmatic

  • Attacks: age at the first attack, frequency, last attack, what triggers them, symptoms between attacks.
  • Severity: emergency visits, admissions, intensive care or ventilation (risk of a fatal attack), courses of oral steroids.
  • Control: night waking, missed school, limitation of play, reliever use per week.
  • Treatment: reliever and controller, dose, inhaler technique and spacer, adherence, response.

Background

  • Past: eczema, allergic rhinitis, recurrent chest infections, measles or pertussis
  • Birth: prematurity, ventilation (chronic lung disease)
  • Immunization: BCG, pertussis, measles
  • Family: asthma, eczema, hay fever (atopy); TB contact
  • Social: smoking at home, charcoal or wood smoke, animals, dust, crowding
  • Development and school: days missed

Asthma in the history
Recurrent night cough and wheeze+triggers: exercise, dust, cold+eczema, family atopy=Bronchial asthma

Which past admission marks an asthmatic child at high risk?
ANSWERAdmission to intensive care or ventilation for asthma.
Which family history supports asthma?
ANSWERAtopy: asthma, eczema or allergic rhinitis in parents or siblings.

Convulsions

The first question in a convulsion history is whether the event was a seizure at all (and not a breath-holding attack, rigor or faint). The next is whether it was provoked (fever, meningitis, low glucose or sodium) or unprovoked (epilepsy), because provoked seizures are treated by treating the cause.

Looking for the cause

InfectionFever, headache, vomiting, neck stiffness, rash (meningitis), malaria; recent diarrhoea (salt imbalance)
Birth historyBirth asphyxia (did not cry at once), neonatal jaundice, prematurity, neonatal fits
DevelopmentDelay (cerebral palsy) or regression (degenerative disease)
FamilyFebrile convulsions or epilepsy in siblings or parents; consanguinity
DrugsAntiepileptic name, dose, adherence, side effects; poisoning
Effect on lifeSchool performance, injuries, restrictions, the family's fears
Exam trap
  • A simple febrile convulsion is generalised, lasts under 15 minutes, does not recur in 24 hours, and occurs between 6 months and 5 years; a focal, long or repeated seizure is complex.
  • A child under 12 months with a convulsion and fever needs a lumbar puncture, because meningitis may show no neck stiffness at this age.

Simple febrile convulsion

What makes a febrile convulsion complex?
ANSWERFocal onset, duration over 15 minutes, or recurrence within 24 hours.
Which three phases of the attack are asked about?
ANSWERBefore (trigger, aura), during (type, duration) and after (postictal state).

Bleeding tendency

SiteSkin and mucosa (petechiae, purpura, nose and gum bleeding) = platelet or vessel disorder · joints and muscles (haemarthrosis, haematoma) = clotting factor disorder
Onset and provocationSince birth or recent · spontaneous or after trauma, tooth extraction or surgery
QuantityNumber of pads or cloths, need for transfusion, symptoms of anaemia (fatigue, breathlessness)
Recent illnessA viral infection 1–3 weeks before = ITP; fever, bone pain, weight loss = leukaemia; rash and joint pain = HSP
Past historyBleeding after circumcision, at the vaccination site, from the umbilical cord, cephalhaematoma at birth
FamilyMale relatives on the mother's side (haemophilia, X-linked) · both sexes (von Willebrand disease) · consanguinity
DrugsAspirin, NSAIDs, anticoagulants
Best next step
  • Investigations: CBC with platelet count and blood film, bleeding time, PT and APTT, factor VIII and IX assays, von Willebrand factor and ristocetin cofactor.
  • Management: supportive care (pressure, no aspirin, no IM injections); haemophilia A: factor VIII; von Willebrand disease: desmopressin (DDAVP), vWF concentrate or cryoprecipitate.

Generalised body swelling

Where it startedFace and eyelids in the morning (renal) · feet first (cardiac, malnutrition) · abdomen first (liver)
UrineAmount (oliguria), frothy urine (protein: nephrotic), tea- or cola-coloured urine (nephritic)
Preceding infectionSore throat 1–2 weeks or skin infection 3–6 weeks before = post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis
Other systemsBreathlessness, orthopnoea (heart failure, fluid overload) · jaundice (liver) · headache, blurred vision, fits (hypertension) · diarrhoea and poor diet (malnutrition)
Past and drugsPrevious attacks and steroid courses, relapses, response

Red urine

  • Colour and timing: red at the end of urination with dysuria = schistosomiasis (swimming in canals); smoky urine with oedema = glomerulonephritis.
  • Exclude false haematuria: beetroot, rifampicin, haemoglobinuria after fava beans (G6PD) or after malaria treatment.
  • Ask about: trauma, loin pain and fever (UTI, stones), bleeding elsewhere, a flank mass (Wilms tumour), deafness in the family (Alport syndrome).

Joint pain and suspected rheumatic fever

JointsLarge joints, migrating from one to another, very painful, quick response to aspirin = rheumatic fever · one hot joint with fever = septic arthritis
Preceding infectionSore throat 2–4 weeks before, treated or not
HeartBreathlessness, palpitations, chest pain, oedema (carditis, heart failure)
Other criteriaAbnormal involuntary movements and clumsy writing (Sydenham chorea), rash, nodules
ProphylaxisPrevious attacks; benzathine penicillin every 4 weeks: regular or missed?
