The dehydration station asks the student to examine a child with diarrhoea, classify the dehydration and choose the treatment plan. The classification follows IMCI: two or more signs in a row place the child in that row, and the treatment plan follows directly from the class.

Signs and how to elicit them

General conditionAlert, restless and irritable, or lethargic or unconscious (call the child, shake gently)
Eyes and tearsSunken eyes (ask the mother whether they look different), tears when crying
Fontanelle and mouthSunken anterior fontanelle (infant, sitting up); dry mouth and tongue
DrinkingOffer water or ORS: drinks normally, drinks eagerly (thirsty), or drinks poorly or cannot drink
Skin pinchPinch the skin of the abdomen halfway between the umbilicus and the side, in the long axis of the body, for one second, then release: goes back immediately, slowly, or very slowly (more than 2 seconds)
CirculationPulse rate and volume, capillary refill (press the nail bed for 5 seconds; normal ≤ 2 seconds), cold hands, BP

IMCI treatment plans

Plan A (home)
  • Extra fluid: ORS after each loose stool, 50–100 ml under 2 years, 100–200 ml at 2 years or more; continue breastfeeding
  • Zinc for 10–14 days
  • Continue feeding
  • When to return: drinking poorly, blood in the stool, getting sicker, fever
Plan B (facility)
  • ORS 75 ml/kg over 4 hours by cup and spoon; more if the child wants more
  • Vomiting: wait 10 minutes, then continue more slowly
  • Reassess after 4 hours and reclassify
Plan C (IV)Ringer lactate 100 ml/kg: under 12 months 30 ml/kg in 1 hour then 70 ml/kg in 5 hours; 12 months and older 30 ml/kg in 30 minutes then 70 ml/kg in 2½ hours; repeat the first 30 ml/kg once if the radial pulse is still weak; ORS 5 ml/kg/hour as soon as the child can drink

Monitoring the response

  • The first signs of improvement are a stronger pulse, a better level of consciousness and the passing of urine.
  • Reassess every 1–2 hours on Plan C; if the signs do not improve, give the drip faster.
  • Signs of overhydration (puffy eyelids, fast breathing, crackles) mean the fluid is slowed or stopped.

Complications of dehydration

  • Hypovolaemic shock and acute kidney injury
  • Metabolic acidosis (deep breathing)
  • Hypokalaemia (weakness, ileus, arrhythmia)
  • Hyponatraemia or hypernatraemia (convulsions)
  • Hypoglycaemia
  • DIC, renal vein thrombosis
  • Malnutrition after repeated episodes

Why the usual signs fail

In a child with SAM the usual signs of dehydration are unreliable: a wasted child has a slow skin pinch and sunken eyes without dehydration, and a child with oedema may be dehydrated with full tissues. Dehydration is therefore diagnosed mainly from the history of recent watery diarrhoea, a recent change in the eyes noticed by the mother, thirst and poor urine output.

Signs to use

  • Recent sunken eyes (the mother says they changed), thirst, reduced urine, cold hands, weak fast pulse.
  • Where a skin pinch is used, it is done over the chest above the nipple, where some fat remains; capillary refill is checked on the thumb nail.
  • Shock in SAM: lethargy or unconsciousness with cold hands, slow capillary refill and a weak fast pulse; septic shock is commoner than dehydration shock.
WHO 2023 · rehydration in SAM
  • ReSoMal orally or by nasogastric tube, slowly: 5 ml/kg every 30 minutes for 2 hours, then 5–10 ml/kg/hour for 4–10 hours, alternating with F-75.
  • IV fluid only for shock: 15 ml/kg over 1 hour of Ringer lactate with 5% glucose (or half-strength saline with 5% glucose); if the child improves, repeat once and then give ReSoMal; if not, treat as septic shock.
  • Stop when the child is no longer thirsty and passes urine, or if the pulse and breathing rise, the liver enlarges or the eyelids become puffy (fluid overload).
Exam trap
  • Standard ORS and Plan C are not used for a child with SAM, because the sodium load and fast fluid cause heart failure; ReSoMal has less sodium and more potassium.
  • Diuretics are never given for the oedema of SAM.

Where the child with SAM is treated

CMAMCriteria
Stabilisation centre (admit)
  • Any medical complication: pneumonia, severe dehydration, shock, hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, severe anaemia, convulsions, lethargy
  • Failed appetite test
  • Oedema +++, or oedema with severe wasting
  • Infant under 6 months who is not feeding well
Outpatient therapeutic programme (OTP)SAM with a good appetite and no complication: RUTF at home, amoxicillin, weekly visits
  • Appetite test: the child is offered RUTF in a quiet place with the mother; a child who eats the required amount passes, a child who refuses or eats too little fails and is admitted.
  • The appetite test is the best single sign of a complicated SAM, because loss of appetite reflects severe infection or metabolic disturbance.
Model presentation

Musa is an 18-month-old boy with SAM with oedema (oedema ++, MUAC 11.8 cm), some dehydration from 5 days of watery diarrhoea, hypothermia (axillary 34.8 °C), oral thrush and Bitot's spots. He was weaned at 3 months onto diluted cow's milk. He fails the appetite test, so he is admitted to the stabilisation centre for warming, ReSoMal, F-75, antibiotics and vitamin A.
