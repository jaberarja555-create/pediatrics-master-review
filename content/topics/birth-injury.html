Birth injuries are injuries to the baby caused by the forces of delivery. Most are minor and heal alone, but a few (subgaleal haemorrhage, intracranial bleeding) can be fatal, and nerve injuries can leave a weak arm. Recognising which swelling or palsy is harmless avoids needless treatment. The main risk factors are macrosomia (IDM), shoulder dystocia, breech delivery and instrumental delivery (forceps, vacuum).

Nerve injuries

Erb palsy (C5–C6)The commonest, from traction on the neck in shoulder dystocia. Arm adducted, internally rotated, elbow extended, forearm pronated, wrist flexed: "waiter's tip". Moro absent on that side but grasp present (hand muscles are supplied by C8–T1); may have phrenic palsy (C4)
Klumpke palsy (C8–T1)Rare, from traction on an abducted arm (breech). Claw hand and weak small hand muscles; grasp absent, Moro present; Horner syndrome (ptosis, small pupil) if the T1 sympathetic fibres are injured
ManagementGentle passive physiotherapy from 1 week (after the swelling settles) to prevent contractures. Monitoring: return of shoulder and elbow movement is the first sign of recovery; most recover by 3 months; refer for nerve surgery if biceps has not recovered by 3–6 months
Facial palsyPressure from forceps or the sacrum. The eye does not close, the nasolabial fold is flat, and the mouth pulls to the normal side when crying; artificial tears protect the cornea; recovers in weeks
Phrenic nerve (C3–C5)Respiratory distress; raised hemidiaphragm on X-ray (paradoxical movement on ultrasound)

Fractures and other injuries

  • Clavicle: the commonest fracture (shoulder dystocia). The baby does not move the arm, the Moro is absent on that side, with crepitus and a lump (callus) at 1 week. It heals alone: gentle handling or pin the sleeve to the vest for 1–2 weeks
  • Humerus or femur fracture (breech); skull fracture and intracranial haemorrhage (subdural, subarachnoid) after difficult delivery
  • Sternomastoid tumour: firm lump in the sternomastoid at 2–3 weeks → torticollis (head tilts to the same side); stretching physiotherapy

Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH)

  • Unstable or dislocated hip from a shallow acetabulum; the left more often (it lies against the mother's spine in utero)
  • Risk factors: female (6 : 1, maternal hormones loosen the ligaments), breech, first baby, family history, oligohydramnios (tight space), tight swaddling with legs straight
  • Newborn tests: Barlow pushes a dislocatable hip out, backwards; Ortolani brings a dislocated hip back in with a "clunk"
  • Later signs: limited abduction, asymmetrical thigh creases, short leg (Galeazzi sign: one knee lower), limp or waddling gait when walking
  • Ultrasound of the hip up to 4–6 months (screen babies at risk at 6 weeks); X-ray after 4–6 months, once the femoral head has ossified
  • Treatment: Pavlik harness (hips flexed and abducted, which centres the head in the acetabulum) under 6 months; closed reduction and hip spica at 6–18 months; open surgery later
  • Monitoring: ultrasound every few weeks in the harness; a stable, centred hip by 3–6 weeks means success; still dislocated → stop the harness and reduce under anaesthesia

Congenital talipes equinovarus (club foot)

  • About 1 in 1000 births; boys 2 : 1; half bilateral; may go with spina bifida, DDH and arthrogryposis
  • Foot points down (equinus) and inwards (varus), sole faces inwards; it cannot be corrected to neutral
  • Positional talipes (from intrauterine position) corrects fully with gentle pressure: physiotherapy only
  • Fixed club foot: Ponseti method from the first weeks, while the ligaments are still soft: weekly plaster casts, small Achilles tenotomy, then a foot-abduction brace to 4 years (stopping the brace early is the main cause of relapse)
Exam trap
  • A hip click without movement of the head is usually a harmless ligament click; the abnormal sign is a clunk (Ortolani).
  • Flexible flat feet, positional talipes and mild tibial torsion resolve on their own; fixed club foot does not.
  • Hip X-ray is unreliable before 4–6 months (the femoral head is not yet ossified): use ultrasound.
