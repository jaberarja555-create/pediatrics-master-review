Tuberculosis (Mycobacterium tuberculosis) is caught from an adult with infectious pulmonary TB, usually in the household. Young children (under 5 years) and those with HIV or malnutrition progress quickly to severe disease (miliary, meningitis). Management follows the WHO 2022 guidelines for children and adolescents.

Forms of childhood TB

TB infectionPositive Mantoux or IGRA, no symptoms, normal X-ray → TB preventive treatment
Primary pulmonary TBGhon focus + enlarged hilar nodes (primary complex) · nodes may compress a bronchus (wheeze, collapse)
Miliary TBBlood spread: very ill child, fever, hepatosplenomegaly, tiny nodules all over both lungs, often with meningitis
TB meningitisSlow onset over 2–3 weeks: irritability, vomiting, headache → neck stiffness, cranial nerve palsy, coma · CSF: lymphocytes, very high protein, low glucose
Lymph node TBCommonest extrapulmonary form: painless, matted cervical nodes, may discharge (scrofula)
OthersSpine (Pott disease: gibbus, paraplegia) · bone and joint · abdominal (ascites, doughy abdomen) · pericardial

When to suspect TB

  • Cough > 2 weeks, fever, weight loss or failure to thrive (SAM not recovering), night sweats, fatigue
  • Contact with an adult with TB · pneumonia not responding to antibiotics · a lump in the neck

Investigations

BacteriologyXpert MTB/RIF Ultra (rapid PCR, also shows rifampicin resistance) on sputum, gastric aspirate, induced sputum, nasopharyngeal aspirate or stool · smear and culture · only 30–40% of children are confirmed
Tuberculin skin testMantoux 5 TU intradermal, read at 48–72 h · positive: induration ≥ 10 mm (≥ 5 mm in HIV or SAM) · shows infection, not disease · false negative in SAM, HIV, measles, severe TB
Chest X-rayHilar or mediastinal nodes, miliary pattern, consolidation, cavity, effusion
OthersNode biopsy or FNA · CSF in suspected meningitis · HIV test for every child with TB · treatment-decision algorithms (WHO) allow treatment without confirmation

ANSWERSevere malnutrition, HIV, measles (also severe TB, steroids).

Treatment of drug-susceptible TB (WHO 2022)

Non-severe TB (3 months–16 years)4 months: 2HRZ(E) / 2HR · non-severe = peripheral node TB, or lung TB in one lobe without cavity or miliary pattern, not complicated
Severe pulmonary or other TB6 months: 2HRZE / 4HR
TB meningitis, bone and joint TB12 months: 2HRZE / 10HR (or a 6-month intensive regimen) · dexamethasone or prednisolone for 4–8 weeks in TB meningitis and pericarditis
Daily dosesIsoniazid (H) 10 mg/kg · rifampicin (R) 15 mg/kg · pyrazinamide (Z) 35 mg/kg · ethambutol (E) 20 mg/kg · child-friendly dispersible fixed-dose tablets by weight band · pyridoxine with isoniazid in malnourished or HIV-positive children
Drug side effectsIsoniazid: hepatitis, peripheral neuropathy · rifampicin: orange urine and tears, hepatitis, drug interactions · pyrazinamide: hepatitis, arthralgia · ethambutol: optic neuritis (safe at the right dose)

Give treatment under direct observation (DOT) · weigh monthly and adjust doses · test for HIV · treat malnutrition · rifampicin-resistant TB: refer for second-line drugs.

Prevention

  • BCG at birth: protects against TB meningitis and miliary TB in young children, less against lung TB
  • Contact tracing: screen every child living with an adult with TB
  • TB preventive treatment for exposed children with no disease (especially < 5 years and HIV-positive): 3HR (3 months isoniazid + rifampicin) or 6H (6 months isoniazid)
Exam trap
  • Exclude active TB (symptoms, X-ray) before giving preventive treatment, or resistance will develop.
  • Orange urine on rifampicin is harmless; warn the family.
