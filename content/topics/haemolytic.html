Haemolytic anaemia is anaemia from shortened red cell survival (normal 120 days) that the marrow cannot make up for. The marrow works harder, so the reticulocyte count is high; the broken haemoglobin gives unconjugated jaundice.

Red cell defects (inherited)Haemoglobin: thalassaemia, sickle cell disease (8.2) · membrane: hereditary spherocytosis, elliptocytosis · enzyme: G6PD deficiency, pyruvate kinase deficiency
Outside the red cell (acquired)Immune (Coombs positive): haemolytic disease of the newborn (3.5), autoimmune haemolytic anaemia, wrong blood transfusion · non-immune: malaria (4.2), sepsis, HUS and DIC (fragmented cells), burns, Wilson disease, hypersplenism

Features of chronic haemolysis

  • Pallor and jaundice together · dark urine only when haemolysis is intravascular (G6PD, malaria, transfusion reaction: haemoglobinuria)
  • Splenomegaly (± liver): extramedullary blood making and red cell destruction
  • Marrow expansion: frontal and parietal bossing, prominent maxilla, depressed nasal bridge (haemolytic face) · hair-on-end skull X-ray
  • Pigment gallstones · growth delay · family history of anaemia, splenectomy or gallstones
  • Tests: low Hb · reticulocytes high · unconjugated bilirubin high · LDH high · haptoglobin low · urine urobilinogen high, stool stercobilinogen high · film shows the cause (spherocytes, target cells, sickle cells, bite cells, fragments) · direct Coombs test: positive in immune haemolysis

Why are reticulocytes high in haemolysis but low in an aplastic crisis?
ANSWERIn haemolysis the marrow makes more red cells to replace those destroyed; in an aplastic crisis parvovirus stops red cell production.

Thalassaemias are autosomal recessive disorders with too little of one globin chain (unlike SCD, where the chain is abnormal). The cells are microcytic and hypochromic; the unpaired chains damage red cell precursors in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis) and cause haemolysis.

β-thalassaemia major (Cooley anaemia)Two abnormal β genes (chromosome 11) · almost no HbA · severe anaemia from 6–12 months (HbF falls) · transfusion dependent
β-thalassaemia intermediaModerate anaemia (Hb 7–10 g/dl) · presents later · occasional transfusion
β-thalassaemia trait (minor)One gene · no or mild anaemia, very low MCV with a high RBC count · HbA₂ > 3.5% · often mistaken for iron deficiency; no treatment, no iron unless deficient
α-thalassaemia (4 α genes, chromosome 16)1 deleted: silent carrier · 2: α-trait (microcytosis, normal electrophoresis, diagnosed by DNA) · 3: HbH disease (β₄) · 4: Hb Bart's hydrops fetalis (γ₄), death in utero

β-thalassaemia major: clinical features

  • Progressive pallor from the second half of the first year, failure to thrive, poor feeding, mild jaundice
  • Massive splenomegaly and hepatomegaly
  • Thalassaemic face: frontal bossing, prominent maxilla, protruding upper teeth, depressed nasal bridge · hair-on-end skull · thin bones, fractures
  • Iron overload (transfusions + more gut absorption): heart (cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia: commonest cause of death), liver (cirrhosis), endocrine (short stature, delayed puberty, diabetes, hypothyroidism, hypoparathyroidism), bronze-grey skin
  • Transfusion infections (hepatitis B and C, HIV) · gallstones · hypersplenism

Investigations

  • CBC: severe microcytic hypochromic anaemia (Hb 3–7 g/dl) · film: target cells, nucleated red cells, anisocytosis and poikilocytosis · ferritin and serum iron high
  • Hb electrophoresis (diagnostic): HbF very high (often > 90%), HbA absent or low, HbA₂ normal or high · parents: β-trait · prenatal diagnosis by chorionic villus sampling
Exam trap
  • In thalassaemia ferritin is high (iron overload), not low: never give iron to a child with thalassaemia major.
  • β-thalassaemia trait is diagnosed by a raised HbA₂; α-trait has a normal electrophoresis and needs DNA testing.
  • β-thalassaemia major is silent at birth: HbF carries oxygen until about 6 months.

What is the commonest cause of death in transfusion-dependent thalassaemia?
ANSWERHeart failure and arrhythmia from iron overload (cardiac haemosiderosis).
Regular transfusionStart when Hb is below 7 g/dl twice (or with poor growth, facial changes) · packed cells 10–15 ml/kg every 2–5 weeks · keep the pre-transfusion Hb 9.5–10.5 g/dl · this allows normal growth and stops marrow expansion (no bone changes) and big spleen · leucodepleted, matched blood; vaccinate against hepatitis B
Iron chelationStart after 10–20 transfusions or when ferritin > 1000 µg/L · deferasirox 20–40 mg/kg/day orally once daily (check creatinine, liver enzymes) · desferrioxamine 20–40 mg/kg SC by pump over 8–12 hours, 5–7 nights a week (eye and hearing checks) · deferiprone orally (agranulocytosis: weekly neutrophil count) · follow ferritin every 3 months, cardiac MRI
SupportiveFolic acid · no iron, tea with meals reduces iron absorption · endocrine follow-up (growth, puberty, diabetes) · heart and liver checks · dental care
SplenectomyHypersplenism (transfusion need up by more than half, low WBC and platelets) or a huge painful spleen · after 5–6 years · vaccines (pneumococcal, Hib, meningococcal) 2 weeks before, then penicillin prophylaxis
CureStem cell transplant from a matched sibling, best before iron damage
PreventionCarrier screening, premarital and genetic counselling · prenatal diagnosis
TIF 2025 · transfusion-dependent thalassaemia
  • Keep the pre-transfusion Hb at 9.5–10.5 g/dl with transfusions every 2–5 weeks.
  • Start iron chelation early and monitor iron overload in the heart and liver as well as serum ferritin.

G6PD deficiency

BasisX-linked recessive (boys) · the enzyme makes reduced glutathione that protects red cells from oxidant damage · the commonest red cell enzyme defect worldwide · protects against malaria
TriggersFava beans (favism) · infection · drugs: primaquine, dapsone, sulphonamides (co-trimoxazole), nitrofurantoin, high-dose aspirin, quinolones · naphthalene (mothballs)
FeaturesHealthy between attacks · 1–3 days after the trigger: sudden pallor, jaundice, dark (cola) urine, back pain, fever · newborn: severe early jaundice (kernicterus risk, 3.5)
TestsFilm: bite cells, blister cells, Heinz bodies (supravital stain) · haemoglobinuria · G6PD assay: may be falsely normal during the attack (young cells have more enzyme); repeat at 2–3 months
TreatmentStop the trigger · fluids for good urine flow (protect the kidneys) · packed cells 10 ml/kg if Hb < 7 g/dl or falling with haemoglobinuria · give the family a list of drugs and foods to avoid · test G6PD before primaquine (malaria guideline)

Hereditary spherocytosis

BasisUsually autosomal dominant · red cell skeleton defect (spectrin, ankyrin) → round spherocytes trapped in the spleen
FeaturesNeonatal jaundice · anaemia, intermittent jaundice, splenomegaly · gallstones · aplastic crises (parvovirus)
TestsFilm: spherocytes · MCHC high · reticulocytes high · direct Coombs negative · eosin-5-maleimide binding test (best) · osmotic fragility increased
TreatmentFolic acid · transfusion for crises · splenectomy after 6 years in moderate–severe disease (vaccines before, penicillin after) · cholecystectomy if gallstones

Why may the G6PD assay be normal during a haemolytic attack?
ANSWERThe oldest, most deficient cells have been destroyed; young red cells and reticulocytes have more enzyme. Repeat the test 2–3 months later.
