Thalassaemias are inherited (autosomal recessive) defects in the amount of globin chains made. In β-thalassaemia major no or little β-chain is made: excess α-chains damage red cells in the marrow (ineffective erythropoiesis) and the spleen destroys them, causing severe anaemia from 6 months of age and a lifelong need for transfusion.

β-thalassaemia major: clinical features

  • Severe anaemia, FTT, jaundice from 6 months (HbF → HbA switch)
  • Massive hepatosplenomegaly (extramedullary haematopoiesis)
  • Bone marrow expansion: frontal bossing, maxillary overgrowth ("chipmunk face"), dental malocclusion, "hair-on-end" skull X-ray, fractures
  • Labs: Hb 3–7, MCV very low, target cells, nucleated RBCs · electrophoresis: HbF ↑↑, HbA absent or low, HbA₂ variable
  • Iron overload (transfusion + ↑ gut absorption): heart failure and arrhythmia (main cause of death), liver cirrhosis, diabetes, hypogonadism, short stature, hypothyroidism, bronze skin

Treatment of β-thalassaemia major

  1. Regular transfusion every 3–4 weeks to keep pre-transfusion Hb 9.5–10.5 g/dL: normal growth, suppressed marrow expansion · filtered, matched blood
  2. Iron chelation when ferritin > 1000 µg/L (after 10–20 transfusions): deferasirox oral daily, desferrioxamine SC infusion 5–7 nights/week, deferiprone (agranulocytosis) · monitor ferritin, cardiac MRI T2*
  3. Folic acid · no iron · vaccines (hepatitis B), endocrine follow-up
  4. Splenectomy if transfusion need rises (hypersplenism) · after age 5, with vaccines and penicillin
  5. Bone marrow transplant from an HLA-matched sibling: curative
  6. Prevention: carrier screening, premarital counselling (common with consanguinity), prenatal diagnosis

G6PD deficiency

  • X-linked recessive (boys) · common in Sudan · enzyme protects RBCs from oxidant damage
  • Triggers of acute haemolysis: fava beans (ful in some cases), infections, drugs: primaquine, dapsone, sulphonamides (cotrimoxazole), nitrofurantoin, ciprofloxacin, high-dose aspirin, naphthalene (moth balls)
  • Neonatal jaundice (may need exchange)
  • Acute intravascular haemolysis 1–3 days after the trigger: pallor, jaundice, dark (cola) urine (haemoglobinuria), back pain, shock, AKI
  • Film: bite and blister cells, Heinz bodies (supravital stain)
  • G6PD assay: can be falsely normal during haemolysis (young cells have more enzyme): repeat after 2–3 months
  • Treatment: stop trigger, fluids (protect kidneys), transfusion if severe · avoid triggers (give a list)

Hereditary spherocytosis

  • Autosomal dominant (usually) · defect of membrane proteins (spectrin, ankyrin) → spherical rigid RBCs destroyed in the spleen
  • Anaemia, intermittent jaundice, splenomegaly, gallstones · neonatal jaundice · aplastic crisis with parvovirus
  • Film: spherocytes · ↑ MCHC · ↑ retics · Coombs negative (unlike ABO/autoimmune) · EMA binding test (osmotic fragility older)
  • Treatment: folic acid, transfusion when needed, splenectomy in severe disease (after 6 years; vaccines + penicillin) ± cholecystectomy
