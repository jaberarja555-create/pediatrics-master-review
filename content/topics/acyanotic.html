In left-to-right shunts blood passes from the high-pressure left side to the right side, so the lungs receive too much blood. The child is pink but breathless, with recurrent chest infections, poor feeding and heart failure in large defects. Obstructive lesions (coarctation, aortic and pulmonary stenosis) load the ventricle with pressure instead.

Ventricular septal defect (VSD)

FactsThe commonest CHD (about 30%) · perimembranous (commonest, about 70–80%) or muscular · Down syndrome, trisomy 13 and 18, fetal alcohol
Small VSDNo symptoms, normal growth · loud, harsh pansystolic murmur at the lower left sternal edge, often with a thrill · normal X-ray and ECG · the smaller the hole, the louder the murmur
Large VSDSymptoms from 4–8 weeks (as pulmonary resistance falls): breathless and sweating on feeding, poor weight gain, recurrent chest infections, heart failure (fast breathing, big liver) · active precordium · pansystolic murmur (softer), apical mid-diastolic murmur (high flow across the mitral valve), loud P2
InvestigationsEchocardiography (diagnostic) · X-ray: cardiomegaly, plethoric lungs · ECG: biventricular hypertrophy
Course30–50% of small defects close by themselves (muscular more often) · large defects → pulmonary hypertension → Eisenmenger syndrome if not repaired
VSD: left-to-right shunt

Treatment of VSD

  • Small: reassure, follow up with echo, good dental hygiene (endocarditis risk) · no surgery
  • Large, symptomatic: heart-failure treatment (furosemide, captopril, spironolactone) · high-calorie feeds (NG if needed) · treat chest infections and anaemia
  • Surgical closure (patch, by 3–6 months, or catheter device for some muscular defects) if: failure to thrive or heart failure not controlled by drugs, or pulmonary hypertension
  • Pulmonary artery banding: temporary palliation in multiple defects
Exam trap
  • In VSD the smallest defects produce the loudest murmurs; as pulmonary hypertension develops the murmur becomes softer, not louder.
  • A large VSD causes heart failure at 6–8 weeks, not at birth; a VSD does not cause cyanosis unless Eisenmenger syndrome develops.

Atrial septal defect (ASD)

TypesSecundum (commonest, about 80%, middle of the septum) · primum (lower septum, with a cleft mitral valve: part of AVSD) · sinus venosus · girls more often
FeaturesUsually no symptoms in childhood; found by a murmur · large: tiredness, recurrent chest infections · arrhythmias and pulmonary hypertension in adult life
SignsWide, fixed splitting of S2 (the key sign) · soft ejection systolic murmur at the upper left sternal edge (extra flow across the pulmonary valve, not across the ASD) · tricuspid flow mid-diastolic murmur in large shunts
InvestigationsEcho · X-ray: right atrial and ventricular enlargement, prominent pulmonary arteries, plethora · ECG: right axis deviation, RVH, partial right bundle branch block (secundum) · left (superior) axis in primum
TreatmentMany small secundum defects close in the first years · large (right heart dilated): catheter device or surgical closure at 3–5 years · endocarditis is very rare (low pressure gradient)
ASD: left atrium → right atrium

Atrioventricular septal defect (AVSD)

  • Primum ASD + inlet VSD + one common AV valve · strongly linked with Down syndrome (screen every Down baby with echo)
  • Early heart failure and pulmonary hypertension in infancy · ECG: superior (left) axis · surgical repair at 3–6 months
AVSD: one common AV valve

Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA)

WhoPreterm babies (immature duct, often closes later) · congenital rubella · girls · babies born at altitude
SignsContinuous "machinery" murmur under the left clavicle (systolic only in the newborn) · collapsing, bounding pulses, wide pulse pressure · active precordium · large duct: heart failure, poor growth, chest infections · preterm: apnoea, difficulty weaning from the ventilator
InvestigationsEcho · X-ray: cardiomegaly, plethora · ECG: LVH (RVH only with pulmonary hypertension)
TreatmentPreterm: ibuprofen or indomethacin (prostaglandin inhibitors) or paracetamol in the first weeks, fluid restriction · term baby or older child: the duct will not close on its own → catheter coil or device (or ligation), ideally in the first year, even if small (endarteritis risk)
PDA: aorta → pulmonary artery
Exam trap
  • The murmur of ASD is not produced by blood crossing the defect; it is a pulmonary flow murmur. The key sign is wide fixed splitting of S2.
  • A PDA is closed with an anti-prostaglandin; prostaglandin E1 keeps it open.
  • ECG in PDA shows LVH, not RVH; RVH belongs to ASD, pulmonary stenosis and tetralogy.

Why does a term baby's PDA need closure but a preterm baby's may close alone?
ANSWERIn term babies the duct wall is abnormal and will not close; in preterm babies it is only immature and often closes, helped by ibuprofen.

Coarctation of the aorta

FactsNarrowing of the aorta, usually near the ductus, beyond the left subclavian artery · boys · Turner syndrome · bicuspid aortic valve in many
Newborn (severe)Duct-dependent: well at birth → when the duct closes (days 2–7) sudden shock, heart failure, acidosis, absent femoral pulses · may have pink arms and blue legs
Older child (mild)Often no symptoms · hypertension in the arms, weak, delayed femoral pulses (radio-femoral delay), BP lower in the legs · headache, nosebleeds, leg pain on exercise · systolic murmur at the back between the scapulae
InvestigationsEcho · four-limb BP · X-ray (older child): rib notching (big collateral arteries), "3 sign" · ECG: LVH (RVH in newborns) · CT or MRI
TreatmentNewborn: PGE1 infusion to reopen the duct, inotropes, correct acidosis → surgical repair · older: balloon angioplasty with stent or surgery · treat hypertension (may persist)
ComplicationsHeart failure, persistent hypertension, berry aneurysm with brain haemorrhage, aortic dissection, re-coarctation, endocarditis
