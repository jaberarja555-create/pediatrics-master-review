The liver makes proteins (albumin, clotting factors), stores glucose, clears bilirubin and ammonia. Hepatitis is inflammation of the liver; in children it is most often viral. Liver tests tell the pattern of damage and, more importantly, whether the liver is failing.

Reading liver tests

Hepatocellular damageALT (more liver-specific) and AST high, often in thousands in acute hepatitis · the height does not predict the outcome
Cholestasis (bile flow blocked)Conjugated (direct) bilirubin, ALP and GGT high · pale stools, dark urine, itching
Liver function (synthesis)Prothrombin time / INR: the best guide to severity (rises within a day) · albumin (falls slowly: chronic disease) · glucose (hypoglycaemia in failure)

Causes of hepatitis in children

  • Hepatitis viruses: A and E (faeco-oral), B, C and D (blood, mother to baby)
  • Other infections: EBV, CMV, malaria, typhoid, brucellosis, leptospirosis, sepsis
  • Drugs and toxins: paracetamol overdose, isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, valproate · herbal medicines
  • Metabolic and autoimmune: Wilson disease, autoimmune hepatitis

Hepatitis A and E

SpreadFaeco-oral (water, food, close contact) · common in crowded areas with poor sanitation · hepatitis E: large water-borne outbreaks (camps for displaced people)
IncubationHepatitis A 2–6 weeks (average 4) · infectious for 2 weeks before and 1 week after the jaundice
CourseYoung children: often no symptoms or no jaundice · pre-icteric: fever, malaise, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, right upper pain · icteric: jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, tender hepatomegaly; the child feels better when jaundice appears
DiagnosisAnti-HAV IgM (recent infection) · anti-HAV IgG = past infection or vaccine (immune) · anti-HEV IgM
OutcomeFull recovery, never chronic, no carrier state · rare: acute liver failure, prolonged cholestasis (itching), relapse · hepatitis E is dangerous in pregnancy (high maternal death)
TreatmentSupportive: rest, fluids, normal diet (no need to avoid fat), no paracetamol in excess, no sedatives · admit if vomiting, drowsiness, bleeding or prolonged INR · stay off school 1 week after jaundice starts
PreventionHand washing, safe water, sanitation · hepatitis A vaccine (inactivated, 2 doses 6 months apart)

Why are stools pale and urine dark in hepatitis?
ANSWERLess bilirubin reaches the gut, and conjugated bilirubin spills into the urine.

Hepatitis B

  • DNA virus in blood and body fluids · in children mostly from the mother at birth (highest risk if she is HBeAg-positive), also household contact, unsafe injections, transfusion · incubation 2–6 months
  • Chronic infection depends on age: about 90% if infected at birth, 30% at 1–5 years, under 5% in adults · chronic infection → cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma
  • Extra-hepatic: arthritis and rash (prodrome), membranous glomerulonephritis, aplastic anaemia

WHO 2024 · preventing mother-to-child hepatitis B
  • Every baby: hepatitis B birth dose within 24 hours, then the pentavalent doses (EPI).
  • Baby of an HBsAg-positive mother: also HBIG 0.5 ml IM within 12 hours (other thigh) · test the baby for HBsAg and anti-HBs at 9–12 months.
  • Mother with high viral load or HBeAg-positive: tenofovir from the second trimester · breastfeeding is allowed.

Treatment: acute hepatitis B is supportive · chronic hepatitis B with active disease (raised ALT, high viral load, fibrosis): specialist care, tenofovir or entecavir (WHO 2024) · test the household and vaccinate contacts.

Hepatitis C and D

Hepatitis CRNA virus · blood: transfusion, unsafe injections, dialysis, mother to baby (about 5%) · usually silent; chronic in most (60–80%) · anti-HCV, then HCV RNA (PCR) to confirm active infection (baby of an infected mother: PCR in infancy, anti-HCV after 18 months) · no vaccine · direct-acting antivirals for all from 3 years (WHO 2022): sofosbuvir-based tablets for 12 weeks, cure > 95%
Hepatitis D (delta)Defective virus that needs HBsAg to infect · only with hepatitis B · makes the disease more severe (liver failure, rapid cirrhosis) · prevented by hepatitis B vaccine

Acute liver failure

  • Sudden loss of liver function in a child with no known liver disease: INR ≥ 1.5 with encephalopathy, or INR ≥ 2 even without it, not corrected by vitamin K
  • Causes: hepatitis A (commonest where it is endemic), hepatitis B, paracetamol overdose, drugs (isoniazid, valproate), Wilson disease, herbal remedies
  • Features: deepening jaundice, bleeding, encephalopathy (sleep change, confusion, coma), shrinking liver, ascites · ALT may fall as the liver dies
  • Tests: INR, glucose, ammonia, electrolytes, creatinine, blood gas, blood culture, cause (viral serology, paracetamol level, caeruloplasmin)
  • Treatment (ICU): glucose infusion (hypoglycaemia) · vitamin K · FFP only for bleeding · lactulose · protein moderated · head up, treat raised ICP (mannitol) · no sedatives · N-acetylcysteine for paracetamol · antibiotics for infection · liver transplant

Chronic liver disease and portal hypertension

CausesChronic hepatitis B or C · autoimmune hepatitis (girls; high IgG, ANA, anti-smooth muscle antibodies; steroids + azathioprine) · Wilson disease · biliary atresia (3.5) · alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency · cystic fibrosis
SignsJaundice, palmar erythema, spider naevi, clubbing, growth failure · splenomegaly, ascites, prominent abdominal veins · bleeding · encephalopathy
DecompensationLow albumin, prolonged INR, rising bilirubin, ascites · enzymes may be normal or only mildly raised (few cells left)
Portal hypertensionCirrhosis, or pre-hepatic: portal vein thrombosis (umbilical catheter, neonatal sepsis) and schistosomal periportal fibrosis (S. mansoni): big spleen and varices with normal liver function · variceal bleeding: resuscitate, transfuse carefully, octreotide, antibiotics, endoscopic band ligation · prevention: propranolol, praziquantel for schistosomiasis

Wilson disease (hepatolenticular degeneration)

  • Autosomal recessive (ATP7B): copper is not excreted in bile → builds up in the liver, brain, cornea, kidney
  • Liver (from 5 years): hepatitis, cirrhosis, acute liver failure with Coombs-negative haemolysis · brain (teenagers): tremor, dysarthria, dystonia, falling school performance, psychiatric change · Kayser–Fleischer rings (slit lamp)
  • Low caeruloplasmin (< 20 mg/dl) · high 24-hour urine copper · liver copper on biopsy · genetic test · screen all siblings
  • Chelation: penicillamine or trientine (with pyridoxine for penicillamine) · zinc for maintenance · low-copper diet · transplant for liver failure · lifelong
Exam trap
  • Aspirin is not given to children under 16 with a viral illness (varicella, influenza): Reye syndrome: vomiting, encephalopathy and fatty liver without jaundice.
  • Any child with unexplained liver disease from 3–5 years should be tested for Wilson disease.
