Perinatal asphyxia is failure of gas exchange around birth → hypoxaemia, hypercapnia and acidosis. Brain injury from it is hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy (HIE), a major cause of neonatal death and of cerebral palsy.

Causes and diagnosis

  • Before or during labour (90%): placental abruption, cord prolapse or compression, obstructed or prolonged labour, uterine rupture, maternal hypotension or eclampsia
  • After birth: failure to breathe, severe heart or lung disease, severe blood loss
  • Evidence: cord arterial pH < 7.0 or base deficit ≥ 12 · Apgar ≤ 5 at 10 minutes or still needing ventilation · encephalopathy starting in the first 24–48 h · multi-organ dysfunction

Effects on other organs

Kidney: acute tubular necrosis, oliguria · heart: hypotension, myocardial dysfunction · lungs: PPHN, meconium aspiration · gut: NEC · liver: raised enzymes, clotting failure · blood: DIC, low platelets · metabolic: hypoglycaemia, hypocalcaemia, hyponatraemia (SIADH), lactic acidosis.

Supportive care

BreathingVentilate if needed · keep normal oxygen and CO2 (avoid hyperoxia and low CO2, which reduce brain blood flow)
CirculationTreat hypotension with fluids and inotropes (dopamine) · correct acidosis by restoring perfusion
FluidsRestrict fluids (about 40–60 ml/kg/day on day 1) because of tubular necrosis and SIADH · weigh daily, check sodium and urine output
Glucose, calciumKeep glucose normal (both low and high harm the brain) · check calcium and magnesium
SeizuresPhenobarbital first (see next page) · no prophylactic anticonvulsants
TemperatureAvoid hyperthermia: every degree above normal adds to brain injury
FeedingDelay feeds (risk of NEC), start slowly when stable · nutrition by NGT if suck is weak

Therapeutic hypothermia

  • Criteria: ≥ 36 weeks, evidence of asphyxia, and moderate or severe encephalopathy
  • Started within 6 hours of birth · core temperature 33.5 °C for 72 hours, then slow rewarming
  • Lowers death and disability in moderate–severe HIE
  • Done only in a NICU able to ventilate, monitor and give full intensive care; otherwise keep normal temperature and avoid fever

Prognosis and follow-up

  • Good signs: normal examination and feeding by 1–2 weeks, normal EEG and MRI · bad signs: severe stage, seizures > 24 h, abnormal EEG (burst suppression), basal ganglia injury on MRI
  • Follow up development, hearing and vision · early physiotherapy
Exam trap
  • Cooling is for babies of 36 weeks or more with moderate or severe HIE, started within 6 hours; mild HIE is not cooled.
  • After asphyxia, give less fluid, not more: the kidneys and ADH limit water excretion.

Causes

BrainHIE (the commonest, first 24–48 h) · intracranial haemorrhage, stroke · meningitis, TORCH · brain malformations · kernicterus
MetabolicHypoglycaemia · hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia · hypo- or hypernatraemia · inborn errors · pyridoxine dependency
OthersMaternal drug withdrawal · benign familial neonatal seizures · neonatal tetanus (spasms, not true seizures)

Types and jitteriness

  • Subtle (the commonest): eye deviation, blinking, lip smacking, sucking, cycling movements, apnoea with tachycardia
  • Clonic (focal or multifocal, rhythmic) · tonic (posturing, common in preterm) · myoclonic (bad prognosis)

Management (ILAE 2023)

  1. ABC, oxygen · check glucose at once, then calcium, magnesium, sodium, blood gas, septic screen (LP), cranial ultrasound, EEG
  2. Treat the cause: 10% glucose 2 ml/kg IV then infusion · 10% calcium gluconate 2 ml/kg IV over 10 min with ECG · magnesium sulfate 50% 0.2 ml/kg · antibiotics if meningitis
  3. First-line anticonvulsant: phenobarbital 20 mg/kg IV over 20 min (a further 10–20 mg/kg if needed)
  4. Second line: phenytoin 20 mg/kg IV, levetiracetam (40–60 mg/kg), or midazolam · resistant: pyridoxine 100 mg IV
  5. Stop anticonvulsants before discharge when seizures were due to an acute cause (such as HIE) and have stopped
